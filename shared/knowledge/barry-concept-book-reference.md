## 1. Main principles

1. **Concept over execution, substance over style.** Without a concept there is nothing; craft cannot rescue a weak idea. The book is deliberately illustrated with pencil roughs rather than finished art to prove this. [p001], [p004]
2. **Order: strategy → concept → campaign → execution.** The subtitle ("think now, design later") is about concept before design, but the true order starts with strategy. [p0041] Consumers only see the tip of the iceberg: the execution. [p003], [p005]
3. **One benefit.** Single-minded proposition = single-minded promise = single-minded benefit. Clients will ask for two; saying two weakens both. Great brands hold one proposition for years (Volvo = safe). [p0073] Exception: the "variety proposition", where the variety is itself the single idea (Classic FM's multiple moods). [p0028], [p0037]
4. **A truth.** Besides simplicity, the recurring feature of great advertising is a truth, found at any level: strategy, idea, line, visual, type, tone. [p0074]
5. **Exaggerate, don't contrive.** Exaggeration starts from a truth; contrivance starts from something untrue. Test for people: "Would anyone really do that?" Test for objects: does it start from something that actually happens? [p0048], [p0049]
6. **Good is the enemy of great.** The goal is work people would want to keep, not work that merely communicates. [p002]
7. **Soft sell over hard sell,** but not so soft that nobody knows what is being sold. Aim for both the head (logic) and the heart (emotion). [p003], [p0076], [p0077], [p0078]
8. **Tools, not rules.** The methods are aids that help you reach an idea; they cannot supply it. [p0074]
9. **Advertising is not always the answer.** Sometimes the fix is the product, a memorial, or nothing. [p0075]
10. **(Most) great ideas are timeless.** Art direction dates an ad faster than copy; strip away trendy technique and judge the concept. Barry cites Stavros Cosmopulos: keep "the layouts rough and the ideas fancy." [p001]
11. **Concept = idea.** Barry uses the two interchangeably: what comes after the strategy and before the executions. [p001]
12. **Impossible to ignore.** Captions hold up Western Union's "Ignore a telegram? You can't" as what every ad should achieve. [p0070] (Caption-level; the surrounding body text is unclear.)
13. **Effectiveness = Impact + Branding + Relevance.** A memorable ad whose product is forgotten is wasted; a well-branded one that is wrong for the audience also fails. [p0069]

## 2. Creative methods (as found in the capture)

### 2.1 Strategy methods

| Method | What it is | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategy statement** | Client · Product/Service · Background (what do they currently think of us?) · Competition · Objective/Problem to solve · Media · Target · Proposition · Support points · Tone · Mandatories. One line per heading except support points. | [p0033], [p0034], [p0030] |
| **Three failure points of a woolly brief** | Proposition that isn't single-minded; support points that aren't credible; target audience that isn't defined. | [p0023], [p0030] |
| **Binary briefing** | Four either/or choices: brand share *or* market growth; trial by non-users *or* more use by existing users; USP *or* branding; logical argument *or* mnemonic. | [p0032], [p0083] |
| **Probe the problem** | Go past the surface objective to the specific barriers (blood donation: fear of needles, fainting, time, not knowing it saves lives). Fix one. | [p0083] |
| **New audience = new strategy** | Often half the job is finding a new target (Flowers & Plants Association: buy your own). | [p0081] |
| **Lateral strategy** | Say something people hadn't thought, not the obvious claim (BUPA: move from "we care more" to "you deserve to stay healthy"). | [p0039] |
| **One-sentence strategy + one-sentence idea** | Exercise: separate strategy from idea for any campaign in one line each. Worked cases: Vespa, Mini, Apple "Welcome, IBM. Seriously." | [p0034], [p0038] |
| **One product, several strategies** | Lego worked four ways: "the brain toy for kids" (parents; intelligent tone); a values stance against sex and violence on TV (parents; serious); owning learning and imagination for adults, beyond "a toy for kids" (adults; any tone); "built to last" (parents; better value vs. cheap toys). Each changes competition, target, proposition and tone. Choose one. | [p0021] (cleaned) |
| **Tone as one or two adjectives** | Examples: VW self-deprecating; BT personal, warm; Barnardo's serious, shocking; The Economist intelligent, elitist; Pepperami edgy. Tone can start general, or "any", but once set it holds for every execution. | [p0037] (cleaned) |
| **Research past the brief** | Research more than you need, or you'll finish inside your first impressions. A dull fact can spark the campaign. | [p0019], [p0080] |
| **The specific individual** | Narrow the target to one typical person and "wear their hat". Tight age ranges. | [p0072], [p0081] |

### 2.2 Idea tools (Basic Tools chapter)

| Tool | Core move | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **The Opposite Tool** | Turn anything on its head: strategy (serious category → humor), negative → positive (Guinness waiting), customer above client ("The patient will see you now, Doctor"), unexpected audience, visuals (pregnant man; zebra chases cheetah), phrases (Think small; "Great minds like a think"), art direction (logo top-left, big pack shot). | [p0063], [p0055], [p0057], [p0058], [p0060], [p0054] |
| **Don't tell, show** | Visual beats verbal in the time an ad gets (Weru windows: tiny mower; Palladium: wide entrance, narrow exit). But sometimes words create stronger pictures (Nikon). | [p0066], [p0067], [p0068] |
| **Don't reveal, imply** | Leave the gap for the audience to close, but not so obscure they can't in a few seconds. Start literal, finish lateral. | [p0064], [p0065], [p0067] |
| **Symbols** | Shorthand for an idea; best when a literal symbol is given a twist (foundation kit + decorator's brush for Miss World). | [p0053], [p0052] |
| **Visualization** | Look for the angle nobody has seen (Jaws poster). | [p0053] |
| **Reductionism: do you need people?** | Remove the people and you gain metaphor, symbol, analogy. | [p0051] |
| **Do you need the product?** | Not always; phase pack shots out once the audience can supply them. | [p0050] |
| **Good-simple vs. bad-simple** | Simple-and-boring is not the goal. Dramatize the simple. | [p0071] |
| **KISS** | Simplicity in both benefit and execution. | [p0072] |
| **1-in-10** | Quantity breeds quality. Write 20, 30, 40. Best lines tend to come in the middle to end of the list. | [p0069] |
| **Write first, edit last** | Separate generation from judgment. | [p0069] |
| **One-sentence test** | If you can't say the idea in a sentence it isn't simple enough. | [p0066], [p0068] |
| **Kill your babies** | Many ideas make it easier to drop a favorite. | [p0068] |
| **Overnight test** | Return fresh; the subconscious keeps working. | [p0068] |
| **Avoid clichéd imagery / don't do "ads"** | Visual clichés list (sax from an NYC window, winding mountain road, token minorities, iPod silhouettes); "addy" work copies what's out there. Exception: parody. | [p0052], [p0051] |
| **No "best"** | "Best" destroys differentiation unless you can prove it; say *why* it's better. | [p0054], [p0028] |
| **Find the truth** | See principle 4. | [p0074] |

### 2.3 Headline and line craft

| Method | Core move | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline twist** | Lead the reader one way with a familiar phrase, then pull back with the ending. Hold the twist as late as possible (last word). Cut words after the payoff. A second clause in parentheses reads like an aside. Can twist off the visual. | [p008], [p0026], [p0027], [p007] |
| **Questions: the seven exceptions** | Avoid yes/no questions, because a "no" loses the sale, unless: it's already being asked; it's almost impossible to answer wrongly; it forces thought first; it's rhetorical; it's hypothetical; it's a double meaning; or the ad supplies the answer. | [p0018], [p009], [p0010] |
| **Punctuation** | No exclamation marks in headlines or taglines unless the idea demands it; they sound desperate. | [p008], [p0026] |
| **Consistency of campaign form** | Don't mix headline-only, visual-only and headline+visual in one campaign; but pick the form from the idea, not before it. | [p008], [p0025] |
| **Tone discipline** | Every execution in a campaign must hold one tone (a slightly more slapstick Guide Dogs spot was shot and never aired). | [p0037], [p0080] |

### 2.4 Media craft (print)

| Method | Core move | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Print first** | Hardest discipline; learn it before others. | [p0016] |
| **Poster: 3 seconds / 8 words** | A guideline, not a law (The Economist breaks it). Use the minimum words without hurting the communication. | [p0015], [p0014] |
| **Letter or postcard?** | Quote from Jim Aitchison: decide between copy-driven press and a visual postcard. | [p0011] |
| **Proster** | Visual-led press ad that doubles as a poster. Caution: it has eroded copywriting portfolios. | [p0024], [p0025] |
| **Consecutive ads** | A set read in sequence, with a reveal at the end. | [p0025] |

### 2.5 Evaluation and critique

| Method | Core move | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **SLIP IT** | A great ad makes you **S**mile, **L**augh, is **I**nformative, **P**rovokes, **I**nvolves, or makes you **T**hink, at least one. A merely good ad only communicates the proposition quickly and simply. | [p0071] |
| **"Do you get it?" not "Do you like it?"** | Show it cold, don't explain. Ask what it's saying. Retest a rewrite on someone new. Getting it matters more than liking it. | [p0079] |
| **Great / good / okay / bad / terrible** | Learn to grade, including award winners. | [p0051] |
| **Committee caution** | A zebra is a horse designed by committee. | [p0079] |
| **Safe vs. irreverent** | Barry defends irreverence over patronizing "safe" work, while separating it from juvenile humor (cross-ref to College Humor, unread). | [p0048] |
| **Plagiarism line** | Innocent, subconscious or deliberate. Taking a nugget and building on it differs from lifting strategies, lines or imagery. | [p0042], [p0043] |
| **Barriers to creativity** | Fear of the unknown, fear of looking stupid, premature judgment, attachment, stopping at the first good idea (adapted by Barry from Tom Monahan). | [p0075] |

## 3. A practical framework: developing and evaluating a concept

Organized by CreativeEngine's modes. This is a synthesis, not Barry's structure.

```
STRATEGY            → CONCEPT               → CAMPAIGN / EXECUTION      → EVALUATE
brief · binary       truth · tools · 1-in-10   form · tone · media        get it · SLIP IT · I+B+R
```

### Stage A: Strategy check (before EXPLORE; BriefMachine / Director)

| Question | Fails if |
|---|---|
| Is there **one** benefit? | Two benefits, or "the best" |
| Are the support points **credible**, and does one hold a spark? | Generic claims |
| Is the target **one person** we could describe? | "Adults 18–54" |
| What is the specific **problem** (the barrier, not the sales goal)? | "Increase awareness" with no barrier |
| Where do we sit on the **four binaries**? | Trying to both grow the market and steal share |
| Is there a **lateral** angle, something they haven't thought? | The strategy restates the obvious |

### Stage B: Generate (EXPLORE)

1. Write the truth in one line.
2. Run the tools deliberately: **opposite**, **show don't tell**, **imply don't reveal**, **symbol with a twist**, **remove the people**, **remove the product**, **exaggerate the truth**.
3. Push to 20–40 before judging (1-in-10). Write first, edit last.
4. Start literal, then push lateral.

### Stage C: Develop (DEVELOP)

| Test | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| One-sentence | Idea fits in one sentence, distinct from the strategy sentence |
| Exaggeration vs. contrivance | Starts from something that is true |
| Good-simple | Simple *and* surprising, not just clear |
| Imply balance | Audience closes the gap in ~3 seconds |
| Line craft | Twist lands late; no dead words after it; no "!" |
| Cliché scan | No stock ad imagery; not "addy" |
| Campaign form | One form, one tone across executions; the idea chooses the form |
| Overnight | Survives a fresh look |

### Stage D: Judge (JUDGE)

| Test | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| Cold read | Stranger "gets it" without explanation |
| SLIP IT | Does at least one of smile / laugh / inform / provoke / involve / think |
| Impact + Branding + Relevance | Memorable, attributable, right for audience and product |
| Kill your babies | Nothing survives because it is a favorite |

## 4. When each method helps, with original examples

All examples below are *(original)*: hypothetical brands, written for this reference.

| Method | Use it when | Example *(original)* |
|---|---|---|
| Opposite: negative → positive | The product has an obvious flaw everyone avoids | A slow-cooker brand owns slowness: "Dinner that takes all day. Which is the point." |
| Opposite: phrase | A familiar saying sits near the benefit | Noise-cancelling headphones: "Silence is golden. Now it's also portable." |
| Show, don't tell | The benefit is physical or measurable | Ultra-thin laptop: a laptop slipped into a paper envelope next to letters, no headline |
| Imply, don't reveal | The payoff is funnier or scarier unseen | Hot sauce: an empty glass of milk, knocked over, beside a half-eaten taco |
| Symbol with a twist | A category has a stock symbol | Coworking space: a necktie tied into a noose, cut through with scissors |
| Remove the people | First idea is "person using product" | Running shoe: a worn trail through grass that stops at a doorway |
| Exaggerate (not contrive) | A real user experience can be pushed | Strong coffee: the mug has a seat belt. Contrived version to reject: coffee that makes you fly (not rooted in anything true) |
| Headline twist | Copy-led formats, radio, social captions | Language app: "Learn Spanish in 3 months. Or keep pointing at the menu." |
| Question headline | The audience is already asking | Budget app: "Where did March go?" (the audience asks this; hard to answer "no") |
| Binary briefing | The client wants "everything" | Regional bank with 60% local share: choose market growth (first-time account holders), not stealing from rivals |
| New audience | The market is saturated | Sunscreen for office workers commuting by bike |
| Lateral strategy | Everyone makes the same claim | Mattress: "sleep better" → "the argument starts better in the morning" |
| One-sentence test | Any review | Strategy: "Make frozen food feel like cooking." Idea: "The freezer is the pantry." |
| Cold-read test | Before any presentation | Show the rough to someone outside the team; ask what it says |

## 5. How it complements, qualifies or conflicts with existing knowledge

### Complements

- **`barry-concept-art-direction.md`.** That framework predates this reading; the book **supports** its core: think now/design later [p001], [p004]; roughs over finished art [p004]; single-mindedness [p0073]; visual clichés [p0052]; campaign consistency [p008]. The Execution chapter (§6) also supports its image decisions, type as concept and campaign consistency. The book adds material the framework lacked: the opposite tool, imply-don't-reveal, "do I need people / the product?", SLIP IT, and the cold-read test.
- **`pricken-idea-mechanisms.md`.** Barry's Opposite Tool ≈ Pricken's Reversal; his exaggeration-vs.-contrivance test sharpens Pricken's Exaggeration; "imply, don't reveal" ≈ Omission; symbol-with-a-twist sits between Fusion and Analogy. Both share *truth first, mechanism second* ([p0074] vs. Pricken §1).
- **`hey-whipple-copycraft.md`.** Headline twist, question exceptions, "no best", no exclamation marks and 1-in-10 are copycraft methods in the same tradition.
- **`brief-normalization.md`.** Barry's strategy statement and the three failure points are a compact readiness check. Binary briefing is a useful forcing device BriefMachine does not currently hold.
- **`selling-the-work.md`.** Committee warning [p0079] and "great work needs a great client" [p005] agree with it.

### Qualifies

- **"3 seconds / 8 words"** is a guideline for roadside OOH [p0015], not a universal law. It maps loosely onto thumb-stopping social formats but was not written for them; platform specs in `shared/knowledge/*-ads/` govern.
- **Hard sell vs. soft sell.** Barry favors soft sell for portfolio and brand work [p003]. In performance social, direct response is legitimate; his own "soften a hard-sell ad" exercise [p0078] is the useful bridge: build the offer *into* the idea rather than bolting it on.
- **"Don't show the product"** [p0050] needs qualifying for paid social and new products, where recognition and product-in-frame often matter. Barry himself says new products usually should be shown.
- **Binary briefing** is presented as either/or; real plans sometimes stage both (acquisition first, frequency later). Use it to force a *primary* choice.
- **Left/right brain** framing [p0076], [p0077] is outdated neuroscience. Keep the useful point (reason *and* emotion) and drop the brain model.
- **Dated examples and attitudes.** Several examples are period- and taste-specific (some gender humor, 1960s tobacco). Read them for the mechanism, not as models.

### Conflicts

- **Framework attribution.** Resolved: `barry-concept-art-direction.md` now separates what is verifiable in the book, what is CreativeEngine's own, and what is unread, and adds the book's tools as §14.
- **Quantity rule vs. EXPLORE philosophy.** None real; the 1-in-10 tool and "write first, edit last" match EXPLORE. The only tension is that Barry's "Kill your babies" belongs in convergence, never mid-divergence.
- **"Best" and legality.** Barry says the UK bars "best" claims [p0028]. Treat this as a historical claim from the book, not current legal advice; claim substantiation follows CLAUDE.md's rule (reviewed evidence or explicit approval).

### 6.1 Author's guidance

| Method | Guidance (paraphrased) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Levels of finish** | Thumbnail → loose rough → tight rough → computer comp → finished ad. The first concept critique is a "tissue session." Thumbnails are unclear to anyone who doesn't already know the idea; a tight rough shows the art director's decisions without pretending to be final | [IMG_3162] |
| **Stimulus, not source** | Keep a visual scrapbook. At the execution stage, reference solves the blank canvas; presented loosely, it becomes a mood board for the client | [IMG_3163] |
| **Don't make it look computer-made** | No over-designed "graphic art" using every tool, no pixelated or muddy retouching, unless the idea needs it | [IMG_3163], [IMG_3164] |
| **Logos done properly** | Always reproduced at full quality. Good logos (letter, word or picture form) work at any size and in white, black or color; use white on dark and black on light for contrast | [IMG_3164], [IMG_3165] |
| **Type = brand, message, audience, tone** | Swap test: "Fresh Eggs" in a hand-painted farm sign reads fresh; in a formal serif on a brass plaque it reads clinical. Swap the two and each fails | [IMG_3166] (fragmentary; the example itself is legible) |
| **Type as concept** | Sometimes the type is the idea, not a carrier for words (magazine headline with "stop" and "walk" colored like a crossing signal) | [IMG_3167] |
| **Optical alignment** | Letter shapes (vertical, round, diagonal) make mechanically aligned large type look misaligned; adjust by eye | [IMG_3167] |
| **Typeface restraint** | Be wary of novelty and default fonts; learn a handful of well-made faces | [IMG_3163], [IMG_3167] |
| **Photo or illustration: decided by the idea** | Photography when it must feel real; illustration for the abstract, surreal, charming, or where a photo would be wrong (an illustrated oil-spill bird is less shocking and more charity-ad) | [IMG_3167], [IMG_3168] |
| **The exact visual, not "close enough"** | Find the exact image, shoot or draw it, or composite it, in rising order of effort. A near-miss stock image can change the idea. Avoid cheesy "concept" stock except as spoof | [IMG_3168] |
| **Shoot craft** | Compose around the idea, keep it clean; natural light; shoot wide and many versions; find the right location (not the obvious nearby one); use a real photographer where possible | [IMG_3169] |
| **Grid** | Structure under all good layout. Fewer elements = more freedom; more elements = more need for a grid | [IMG_3169], [IMG_3171] |
| **Begin with a classic layout** | Learn the Krone/DDB layout (dominant visual, headline below, body copy in columns, logo lower right) and its most-to-least hierarchy; then experiment. Steve Dunn: put the time into writing the ad and basic art direction will work | [IMG_3171] |
| **Layout considerations** | Space, balance, mood, tone, rhythm, pitch, weight, texture, type (listed, not expanded in the capture) | [IMG_3171] |
| **Campaign consistency: same yet different** | Executions should differ conceptually but connect visually: typeface, palette and element positions stay constant; logo size stays constant; headline size may vary to keep proportion with white space. Any unmotivated discrepancy reads as a mistake | [IMG_3171], [IMG_3172], [IMG_3173] |
| **Layout twist** | The Opposite Tool applied to layout (Steve Dunn: logo top left, big pack shot, body copy louder than headline), with logic, an implicit grid and consistency | [IMG_3173] |
| **Don't do ads (execution)** | An ad needn't look like an ad; a small difference can make it stand out | [IMG_3173] |
| **Trust your instincts** | Execution is more subjective than concept; if it feels wrong, change it | [IMG_3173] |
| **Compare and contrast versions** | One version = one page; keep versions side by side instead of overwriting one layout | [IMG_3173], [IMG_3175] |
| **Color from idea and brand** | Choose a palette that fits brand, product and idea. Some brands own a color (Kodak yellow, UPS brown, Tiffany green, FT pink, Orange) | [IMG_3175] |
| **Tonal contrast for legibility** | Light+light, mid+mid, dark+dark = least legible; light+dark = most. Watch small type most | [IMG_3175], [IMG_3176] |
