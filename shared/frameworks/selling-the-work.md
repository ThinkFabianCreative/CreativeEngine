# Selling the Work

> CreativeEngine shared framework. Owner `creative-engine-director`. Used by `word-smither` and `design-champ` when preparing work for presentation, and by `brief-machine` when an objection turns out to be a strategy problem.

## Provenance

In-house CreativeEngine framework. It is not derived from a single named book, author or course, and it reproduces no institution's curriculum. It synthesizes ordinary professional practice in creative presentation and client approval.

- Do not attribute its tests, labels or categories to any named source.
- It describes how work survives approval. It is not a persuasion or negotiation manual, and nothing in it licenses pressuring, misleading or manipulating a client.

## Purpose

Every other CreativeEngine framework asks whether the work is good. This one asks whether the good work will survive the people who have to approve it, and what it needs in order to.

```
IS THERE A DEFENSE, OR ONLY A PREFERENCE?
WHO CAN KILL THIS, AND ON WHAT GROUNDS?
WHAT IS LOAD-BEARING, AND WHAT IS NEGOTIABLE?
DID WE EDIT THIS FOR A REAL OBJECTION, OR AN IMAGINED ONE?
```

Two failures, opposite in direction, equally fatal:

- **The undefended idea.** Strong work with no articulated reason to exist, killed by the first person in the room who says "I'm not sure about this."
- **The pre-compromised idea.** Work sanded down in-house against an objection nobody actually made, presented safe and approved instantly because there is nothing left to object to.

This framework fights both. It does not make work more palatable. It makes work defensible.

## What this framework is not

- **Not a reason to lower ambition.** An objection is a thing to answer, not automatically a thing to obey.
- **Not a substitute for the work being good.** A defense cannot rescue an idea that `creative-direction-audit.md` finds ordinary.
- **Not client knowledge.** What *this* client believes, has rejected before, or will not fund lives in `projects/<client>/client/`, per the root `CLAUDE.md` scope rules. This framework supplies method. A client's actual politics are evidence, and CreativeEngine does not invent them.
- **Not a licence to fabricate.** Never invent precedent, effectiveness data, audience research or competitor activity to win a room. See §8.

## 1. The defense is built with the idea

An idea's defense is not written after selection. If it cannot be assembled while the work is being made, that is diagnostic.

For any idea going to a room, it should be possible to state:

```
PROBLEM:      the business or communication problem, as the client would recognize it
IDEA:         one plain sentence
WHY THIS:     what this does that the obvious answer does not
IT BETS ON:   what must be true for it to work
EVIDENCE:     what support actually exists, with its status
COST OF SAFE: what the predictable version costs
```

Two of these do most of the work.

**WHY THIS** must survive the swap test: replace the brand name with a competitor's. If the answer still holds, the defense is generic, and so, probably, is the idea.

**EVIDENCE** includes its author. A plan, audit or deck the agency wrote is our analysis, however well sourced it is internally. Cite it to the client as our read, not as independent support, and never let it stand in for the client's own data.

**COST OF SAFE** is the argument most often left out. A room evaluating a distinctive idea in isolation is comparing it to nothing, so the risk of the idea is the only risk visible. Naming the alternative — an ad indistinguishable from its category, a message this audience has already learned to skip — puts the real choice back on the table. Do not inflate it. "This will be invisible" is a judgment about attention, and it is offered as a judgment, never dressed as data.

## 2. Map the decision, not the meeting

Work is rarely killed by the person it is presented to.

| Role | What they are protecting | What kills work here |
|---|---|---|
| **Day-to-day contact** | Their own standing with their boss | An idea they cannot re-present without help |
| **Budget holder** | Spend against outcome | An idea with no stated mechanism for working |
| **Veto holder** | The brand, or their own judgment | Anything that feels unlike the brand, or unlike their taste |
| **Legal / compliance** | Claims, rights, regulation | A claim outside its ledger status; a depiction implying something untrue |
| **Brand owner elsewhere** | A brand asset the client team uses but does not own: a mascot, a master campaign line, a licensed typeface, a parent brand | An idea whose ownability rests on that asset, presented to people who cannot approve it |
| **The absent stakeholder** | Whatever they will say next week | An idea that reaches them second-hand with no defense attached |

Three consequences:

- **Arm the contact.** Whoever presents this internally, without us present, needs the idea in one sentence and one reason. If it cannot travel in that form, it will be reported inaccurately, and the version that gets decided on is the reported one.
- **Find where the idea's ownability lives.** If it rests on an asset owned by another part of the client organization, the real approver may never be in the room. Decide in advance what happens if permission is refused (§4, ALREADY GONE), and put the permission to the client as a question, not an argument.
- **Send each objection to its own domain.** A legal objection is answered with the claim ledger, not with creative argument. A budget objection is answered with production options, not with a better rationale.

When the client's real decision chain is unknown, say so. Do not invent stakeholders or motives.

## 3. Objection ledger

Objections are rarely literal. Diagnose before answering.

| Said | Usually means | Legitimate response |
|---|---|---|
| "It doesn't feel like us" | A real identity conflict, or unfamiliarity mistaken for wrongness | Check against approved brand sources. If it conflicts, that is a constraint. If it merely differs from what they have run before, name that distinction plainly |
| "Our audience won't get it" | The speaker didn't get it instantly, or is speaking for an audience they are not in | Check comprehension honestly (`barry-concept-art-direction.md` §5 two-second test, 1 + 1 = 3). If it reads, say what the audience is being credited with. If it doesn't, they are right |
| "Where's the product / the logo?" | Visibility anxiety, often from an earlier bad experience | Distinguish presence from prominence. Show where the brand enters and why that moment is stronger than an earlier one |
| "Has anyone done this before?" | Wants the risk transferred to precedent | Precedent is not available for genuinely new work, and saying so is the honest answer. Never invent one |
| "I don't like it" | Preference, sometimes standing in for an unarticulated concern | Ask what it would have to do differently. If it stays preference, it is preference: record it as client knowledge, not as a creative finding (audit §9) |
| "Legal / brand won't approve it" | Sometimes true, often a guess | Check the ledger, the approved legal source and who owns the brand asset in question. An unverified guess is an open question, not a kill |
| "This isn't what we briefed" | The work answers a reframed problem without showing the reframe, or the brief itself has changed or conflicts | Not answerable by defending the idea. Show the brief's own words and the bridge from them to the idea. If briefs genuinely conflict or have changed, that is a scope question for the client and for `brief-machine`, not a creative argument. Where the work covers one job and the brief asked for another, show what covers the other job, or concede the gap |
| "Can we see more options?" | Low confidence in the recommendation, or a presentation that gave no reason to choose | Usually a presentation failure rather than an idea failure. See §5 |
| "Can we make it clearer?" | Something genuinely is unclear, or they want the strategy stated on the page | Fix real ambiguity. Refuse strategy-as-copy (`hey-whipple-copycraft.md` §14) with a specific reason |
| "Let's test it" | Risk deferral | Legitimate where real testing exists. Note what testing can and cannot tell you about unfamiliar work |

Two rules govern the whole table:

- **Answer once.** A point defended and then raised again has been decided against. Continuing to argue converts a creative disagreement into a relationship problem.
- **Concede real ones fast.** An objection that is correct should be conceded immediately and visibly. Credibility spent defending a genuine flaw is not available for the idea that matters.

## 4. Load-bearing vs negotiable

Before presenting, decide what the idea cannot lose. Write it down.

```
LOAD-BEARING:  remove this and the idea is gone
NEGOTIABLE:    can change without damaging the idea
ALREADY GONE:  we would rather withdraw the idea than run it without this
```

Most creative death is incremental: a series of individually reasonable changes, none of which was refused, arriving at work nobody would have approved as a first proposal.

Naming the load-bearing element in advance is what makes the later refusal possible — and makes it specific rather than precious. This is not a negotiating tactic. It is a decision made while judgment is uncompromised, so it can be honored when judgment is tired.

## 5. What to show

- **Present only work you want made.** A "safe option" included for contrast is the option that gets chosen. If a range is genuinely required, every option in it must be defensible.
- **Recommend.** Presenting three ideas of apparently equal standing hands the creative decision to people who are not creative directors. The departments recommend, CreativeDirector selects, and the client approves a selection rather than a menu.
- **Set the problem before the idea.** A room that has not agreed on the problem cannot agree on the solution. Agreement on the problem is the highest-leverage moment in any presentation.
- **Finish appropriate to stage.** Over-rendering an early concept anchors the conversation on details nobody decided (`barry-concept-art-direction.md` §12, audit §8.1). Under-rendering an idea that genuinely depends on craft invites rejection of the wrong thing.
- **Do not explain the idea into the ground.** If it needs a paragraph before it lands, the problem is upstream.

## 6. Pre-compromise

The failure that happens before the room, inside our own department.

Signs:

- An option was removed because "they'll never go for it," with no record of them refusing anything like it.
- The work answers an objection nobody has made.
- The strongest territory from EXPLORE is missing from DEVELOP with no stated reason.
- Everything presented sits comfortably inside what this client has already run.
- The rationale is about approval likelihood rather than about the audience.

Client preference is real evidence and belongs in client knowledge. The failure is not honoring a known preference. The failure is inventing one and then treating it as a constraint.

CreativeDirector's check: **what did we not show, and who decided?** If the answer is "we assumed," the assumption goes back on the table as an explicit decision.

## 7. Losing well

Some ideas are correctly killed. Some are incorrectly killed by people entitled to kill them.

- **Take the decision.** Relitigating a made decision costs more than the idea is worth.
- **Extract what survives** — the insight, the mechanism, the tension — into the project's `work/reviews/`, tied to the version it concerned, so the next round does not rediscover it.
- **Promote carefully.** A rejection becomes client knowledge only through the promotion rules in the root `CLAUDE.md`: deliberately, and stated no more broadly than its evidence. One rejected execution is not "this client hates humor."
- **Do not let a kill lower the assessment retroactively.** Audit §9 keeps quality and preference separate in both directions. Work that was strong and was rejected was strong and was rejected.

## 8. Honesty limits

Non-negotiable. These outrank every technique above.

- No invented effectiveness data, projected reach, engagement or behavior change (audit §10).
- No invented precedent, award history, competitor activity or case studies.
- No invented audience research, quotes or testimonials.
- No claim used outside its ledger status (`brief-normalization.md` §5). A great defense built on an unverified claim is flagged, not deployed.
- No implying internal consensus, client approval or third-party endorsement that does not exist.
- No pressure techniques: manufactured deadlines, implied scarcity of the idea, or framing approval as a test of the client's courage.

Work that needs any of these in order to be approved should not be approved.

## 9. By operating mode

| Mode | Use |
|---|---|
| **EXPLORE** | Do not use. Sellability is not a filter on exploration, and applying it here is precisely how pre-compromise (§6) happens. The only exception is noting a hard legal or claim restriction, so a territory is not built on something prohibited |
| **DEVELOP** | Build the defense (§1) alongside the idea, as a diagnostic: an idea whose WHY THIS will not resolve usually has a conceptual problem, not a presentation problem. Decide load-bearing elements (§4) while judgment is fresh |
| **JUDGE** | Full use. Assemble the defense, run the objection ledger against the actual work, decide what to show and what to recommend (§5), and run the pre-compromise check (§6) |

## 10. Framework output

When useful:

```
IDEA:              [one plain sentence]
PROBLEM:           [as the client would recognize it]
WHY THIS:          [what it does that the obvious answer doesn't]
IT BETS ON:        [what must be true]
EVIDENCE:          [what exists, with status — or "none available"]
COST OF SAFE:      [what the predictable version costs, as a judgment]
LOAD-BEARING:      [what cannot be removed]
NEGOTIABLE:        [what can]
LIKELY OBJECTIONS: [the two or three most probable, with the domain each belongs to]
UNKNOWN:           [what we do not know about the decision chain]
```

Do not produce this wrapper when the user wants finished work and it would make the answer worse. Do not expose hidden chain-of-thought.

## 11. Framework principle

The department's job is not to get work approved. It is to make work worth approving, and then give it an honest chance of surviving the room. An idea nobody can defend was probably never finished. An idea that survived by giving up everything it was about did not survive.
