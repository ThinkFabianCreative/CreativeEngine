# Visual Craft

> CreativeEngine shared framework. Owner `design-champ`. Used by `creative-engine-director` in review, and by `word-smither` when image and copy are composed together.

## Provenance

In-house CreativeEngine framework, synthesizing ordinary professional practice in composition, color, imagery and visual systems as it applies to advertising and brand work. It is not derived from a single named book, designer, school or curriculum.

- Do not attribute its tests, categories or labels to any named source.
- Style names and categories used as examples are descriptions of kinds, not recommendations.

## Purpose

`barry-concept-art-direction.md` decides whether there is a visual idea and in what order it is received. `typography.md` decides what the type does. This framework covers the rest of the craft that makes a decided visual idea land:

```
DOES THE COMPOSITION PUT THE EYE WHERE THE IDEA NEEDS IT?
IS THE STRUCTURE (GRID, SPACE, DENSITY) A DECISION OR A DEFAULT?
DOES COLOR HAVE A JOB?
IS THE IMAGE CRAFTED, OR RETRIEVED?
WHICH VISUAL CODES ARE WE USING, AND ON PURPOSE?
DOES IT HOLD TOGETHER AS A SYSTEM ACROSS FORMATS?
```

It especially fights:

- composition by default: centered, symmetrical, evenly weighted
- color chosen by mood board rather than by job
- imagery that could be stock, or that looks generated
- category codes adopted without anyone deciding to
- a campaign "system" that is one layout resized
- finish that is either careless or overworked

## 1. Craft starts from a decided idea

Before any craft decision, the concept sentence exists (`barry-concept-art-direction.md` §1). Then write:

```
CONCEPT:     one sentence, from Barry §1
CARRIED BY:  visual / verbal / visual-verbal / typographic / behavioral   (Barry §3)
FIRST:       what must be seen first, and why
FEEL:        the one quality the craft must deliver (tension, calm, speed, warmth, authority…)
LOCKED:      brand colors, logo rules, photography rules, mandatories
FORMATS:     every canvas this must work on
```

FEEL is one word or phrase, not three. If it can't be chosen, the concept isn't settled yet.

## 2. Composition

Composition decides where the eye lands, how long it stays and where it goes next.

- **One focal point.** Make it unmistakable through scale, contrast, isolation, position or the one element that breaks the pattern. Two focal points compete; the viewer picks one at random.
- **Scale is argument.** The biggest thing is what the piece is about. If the biggest thing is the least important, the composition is lying.
- **Tension or balance, chosen.** Symmetry says stable, formal, final. Asymmetry says movement, energy, a question. Pick the one the FEEL needs; centered-by-default is a decision nobody made.
- **Crop with intent.** What's left out creates the question. Crops that cut at joints, heads or product edges by accident read as mistakes; crops that cut on purpose read as confidence.
- **Edges are active.** Elements near an edge create tension and imply space beyond the frame. Use it or keep a clear margin; don't drift halfway.
- **Space directs.** Negative space should push toward the focal point or create a pause. Space that points nowhere is leftover.
- **Density is a choice.** Sparse is not automatically sophisticated, and rich is not automatically noisy. Density matches the idea and the viewing condition.

Tests:
- **Squint:** what survives when detail disappears? That's the composition.
- **Thumbnail:** shrink to feed-thumbnail size. Is the focal point still first?
- **Cover:** hide each element in turn. If nothing is lost, it doesn't belong.
- **Mirror:** flip horizontally. If it suddenly feels better or worse, the original balance was accidental.

## 3. Structure: grids, alignment and space

- **A grid is a decision about rhythm.** Columns, margins and gutters set how the eye moves and how elements relate. Choose them for the content and formats, not from a preset.
- **Grids matter most in systems.** Carousels, campaign sets, multi-format rollouts and anything with repeated units need a shared structure so pieces read as one family. A single hero image may need no visible grid at all.
- **Align to fewer lines.** Every new alignment edge is visual noise. Most layouts need two or three.
- **Optical over mathematical.** Round shapes, punctuation, italic type and pointed forms need to overshoot the grid to look aligned. Trust the eye, then check it at real size.
- **Margins are content.** Consistent outer margins make a set feel designed; inconsistent ones make it feel assembled.
- **Break the grid once, on purpose.** One element that breaks structure becomes the focal point. Several become chaos.
- **Spacing has a scale.** Use a small set of spacing values and repeat them. Arbitrary gaps are the most common tell of an unconsidered layout.

## 4. Color

**Color has a job before it has a hue.** Name it:

| Job | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Signal** | Makes the brand recognizable before the logo is read |
| **Direct** | Pulls the eye to the one thing that matters |
| **Mean** | Carries the idea (warning, warmth, money, nature, night) |
| **Set temperature** | Establishes mood across the whole frame |
| **Separate** | Distinguishes levels, states or units in a system |

Craft:

- **Proportion before palette.** How much of each color matters more than which colors. A dominant, a secondary and one accent, in clearly unequal amounts, reads as intended. Equal amounts read as undecided.
- **Value before hue.** Convert to grayscale. If the focal point doesn't hold, the problem is contrast of light and dark, and changing the hue won't fix it.
- **One accent, used sparingly.** Accent color loses power every time it's used. If everything is highlighted, nothing is.
- **Photography is part of the palette.** The grade (warm or cool, contrast, saturation) either belongs to the brand palette or fights it. Grade to the system.
- **Locked brand colors still leave freedom:** proportion, value range, which color leads, how photography is graded around them, and what is left out.
- **Accessibility contrast is a requirement, not a preference.** Check text and essential graphics at their real size.
- **Medium changes color.** Screens vary, print shifts, DOOH runs bright and viewed in daylight, feed dark mode inverts the surroundings. Check where it will actually be seen.

## 5. Imagery craft

Barry §6 decides what the image argues. This section is how it's made so the argument lands.

**Photography direction.** Specify, don't hope:

- **Light:** direction, hardness, source and time of day. Light sets the FEEL faster than anything else.
- **Viewpoint and distance:** eye level, above, below, close, far. Viewpoint decides whose experience it is.
- **Lens character:** compressed or wide, shallow or deep focus. Each makes a different claim about intimacy and context.
- **Casting, styling and props:** specific, real, lived-in. One precise detail makes a scene believable; generic details make it stock.
- **Set dressing:** remove everything that isn't the idea or its context. Then add back one thing that makes it true.
- **Grade:** consistent across a set, matched to the palette (§4).
- **Space for type:** if type sits on the image, compose the image for it: a quiet area, the right contrast, no competing detail.

**Illustration.** Choose a style by what the idea needs (abstraction, humor, clarity, speed, warmth), not by trend. A style must hold across every execution the campaign needs.

Vocabulary for styles, angles, lenses, apertures, light setups, film stocks, reference photographers and directors (and how to translate them into prompts), and how the main generation models differ: `DesignChamp/knowledge/image-direction-reference.md`.

**Generated imagery.** Use generation to test a decided idea (Barry §2), never to find one. Before any generated image is shown:

- check hands, text, reflections, shadows, physics and repeated faces
- check that it doesn't imply a real product, place or result that doesn't exist
- follow the governing skill record and any client rule (for example, where a brief requires real people or real photography, generated people are not allowed even in comps)
- label it as a placeholder or generated comp

**Stock.** If stock is used, fix what makes it stock: generic casting, perfect light, empty backgrounds, eye contact with the camera, the "diverse group laughing." Crop, regrade or reject.

**Text on images.** Fix legibility with the image (crop, darken the area, reposition, choose a different frame) before adding a scrim, stroke or shadow to the type.

## 6. Visual codes

Every category and culture has visual codes: the colors, image types, compositions and materials people read as "this kind of thing." Using a code sends a signal whether or not anyone meant it to.

1. **Map the codes.** What does the category look like right now? Its colors, typical imagery, layout habits, materials, props. For a client, use real competitor and category material in the project's evidence. For reference, `DesignChamp/knowledge/brand-design-md/` shows how some brands systematize, not what a client's category looks like.
2. **Decide for each code:**
   - **Adopt** it where it buys credibility or recognition the brand needs.
   - **Subvert** it where breaking it is the idea.
   - **Avoid** it where it makes the brand interchangeable.
3. **Check the reading.** Codes mean different things to different audiences and cultures (a color, a gesture, a symbol, a setting). When the audience differs from the team, check how the code reads to them before relying on it.

The failure is not using codes. The failure is using them without deciding to.

## 7. Visual systems

A campaign or multi-format rollout needs a system: a small set of constants that make every piece recognizable, and variables that let each piece be new.

```
CONSTANT:  palette proportion · grid and margins · type roles · image grade and style · one graphic device (if any)
VARIABLE:  the scene · the crop · the format · the words
```

Barry §8 sets the conceptual premise. This is its visual form.

- **Brand-blind test.** Cover the logo on three executions. Are they recognizably one family, and recognizably this brand?
- **Format test.** Take one execution to the most different formats it must run in (for example feed 4:5, Stories 9:16, a wide banner, a roadside screen). Does the mechanism re-compose, or does the layout just shrink?
- **Device trap.** A recurring graphic device (a shape, a line, a frame) must carry meaning or structure. A device that only signals "same campaign" is decoration.
- **Template is not system.** Identical layouts with the pictures swapped are a template (Barry §8). A system produces different pieces that obviously belong together.

## 8. Finish

The audience perceives finish before they can name it. Refine what they'll actually see:

- consistent edges, radii, stroke weights and spacing values
- clean image edges, cutouts and masks
- no accidental collisions between type, image and interface overlays
- compression and export checked at real size, on the target device
- every placeholder labelled, no stray layers or artboard debris in a presented file

**Stop rule.** Stop when further work adds only ornament or overworking. Craft that doesn't change what the audience perceives is time spent on the file, not on the work.

## 9. Generic visual tells

Barry §10 names concept-level clichés. These are the craft-level tells: signs a visual was retrieved or generated rather than decided.

**Generated-image tells**
- even, sourceless light and plastic skin
- impossible hands, garbled text, melting objects, wrong reflections
- the same face across different people
- over-detailed surfaces with nothing chosen
- glossy sheen on everything

**Layout tells**
- centered stack: headline, subline, button, on every job
- card grid or bento boxes as the default structure
- floating 3D objects or blobs with no job
- glass panels with nothing behind them worth seeing through
- a gradient blob background standing in for an idea

**Color tells**
- the default purple-to-blue "tech" gradient
- teal-and-orange grade on everything
- neon on near-black as instant "future"
- every color at equal strength

**Stock tells**
- eye contact and a smile at camera
- spotless, empty environments
- the handshake, the laptop laugh, the arms-raised summit

Warnings, not bans. A tell used knowingly and visibly turned can be the idea. Count each weakness once, under its most precise diagnosis (`CreativeDirector/AGENT.md`, framework orchestration).

## 10. Real conditions

Judge at the size, distance and context where it will actually be seen:

| Condition | Check |
|---|---|
| Feed at thumb speed | Focal point first at thumbnail size; one read in a glance |
| Vertical full-screen | Key content clear of interface overlays and safe zones (verified specs) |
| Dark-mode feed | The asset's edges and darks don't dissolve into the interface |
| Out of home | Viewing distance, dwell time, daylight and night; simplify until it reads |
| Small display units | Detail disappears; the mechanism must survive at the smallest size |
| Compression | Gradients band, fine texture muddies, dark areas block up |

Type-specific conditions are in `typography.md` §8.

## 11. By operating mode

| Mode | Use |
|---|---|
| **EXPLORE** | Almost none. Roughs, crops and color experiments are thinking tools. Don't apply finish, tells or system tests as filters. Map category codes early if useful, as stimulus |
| **DEVELOP** | Full use on the chosen concepts: composition, structure, color jobs, imagery direction, codes decided, system constants set. Run the format and brand-blind tests |
| **JUDGE** | Does craft deliver the concept, or decorate it? Focal point, color job, image credibility, system coherence, real conditions. Name tells precisely, once |

### 12.1 Figma agent brief

When the work will be built by Figma's agent (or any build agent), translate the decisions above into instructions it can execute without interpretation. The agent can't act on "warm", "premium" or "let it breathe". It can act on sizes, hex values, proportions, exact words, named layers and checks.

**Rules for writing the brief**

- **Numbers, not adjectives.** Every craft decision becomes a value: frame size, margin, column count and gutter, spacing steps, type size ratio, color hex and approximate share of the frame, image area coordinates.
- **Exact words, marked as exact.** Paste every line of copy as it must appear, with typographic apostrophes and marks, and say "do not change the words".
- **One decision per line.** No compound instructions an agent could half-follow.
- **Say where color goes, not just which colors.** "Gold on the word COWBOY only" beats "gold accent".
- **Name layers and components.** Anything that will be swapped later (a proof line, a program label, a photo) becomes a named component or variant.
- **Placeholders are explicit.** Specify their size, fill and label text ("PLACEHOLDER IMAGE - not for external use"). Never let the agent choose or generate an image unless the governing record and the brief allow it.
- **Hard prohibitions as a list.** Effects, imagery, words and claims that must never appear, copied from the brand rules and the project's constraints.
- **Order the work.** Numbered parts, smallest reviewable pass first (one lead execution before every variant).
- **Checks and a report.** Finish with what to verify and ask the agent to report what it built, what it skipped and why, and anything that didn't fit.
- **Attach the governing files.** Reference the project's pack files and state that they win over the prompt if anything conflicts.

**Brief template**

```
Build [internal comps / presentation frames] for [client · project]. Internal only.
Follow the attached [pack files]. If anything below conflicts with them, the attached files win.
Do not change any words. Use typographic apostrophes (’).

SET UP
- New page: "[name + date]"
- Frames: [format name] [W × H] ([count])
- Grid: [columns] columns, [gutter] px gutter, [margin] px outer margin; spacing steps [values]
- Color: [hex] [role] about [share]% · [hex] [role] about [share]% · [hex] accent on [exact element] only
- Type: [face] [weight] (stand-in label if any); sizes [role: px] …; tracking [values]
- Logo: [component name], [color on ground], [position]
- Components: [layer names that must be swappable]

PART 1 · [lead execution]
  [Frame / card 1]  [exact words] · [color rule] · [image: area, placeholder label, crop note]
  [Frame / card 2]  …
PART 2 · [variants or other formats, only after Part 1]
  …

DO NOT
- [effects] · [imagery] · [words/claims] · [layout habits] · [killed directions]

CHECKS
- Focal point is first at thumbnail size · one accent per frame · contrast passes at real size
- Key content inside safe zones for [placement] (cite spec file)
- All placeholders labelled · layer names as specified

WHEN DONE
List what you built, what you skipped and why, and any line that didn't fit.
```

Client facts (colors, words, rules) come from the project's files and are written into the brief, never into this framework. Save each brief in the project's `work/figma-agent-pack/`, numbered and versioned.

## 13. Framework principle

Craft is the idea made perceptible. Every visual decision either sharpens what the audience receives or blurs it, and nothing is neutral. When the craft is right, nobody notices it; they notice the idea.
