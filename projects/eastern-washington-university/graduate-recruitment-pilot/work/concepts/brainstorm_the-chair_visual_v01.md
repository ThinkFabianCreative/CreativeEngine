## The system idea in one line

Chairs are how work ranks people: the stool, the task chair, the manager's chair, the director's chair, and, at the bottom, no chair at all. EWU's chair system shows the seat she's been given, and the red band shows where she stands instead.

**System constants (carried from The Room):** reverse shot at standing eye height; red floor band about 8% of frame height, the place where you stand; role-credit caption top left, written like a stage programme or an inventory tag; real EWU rooms in final work; no regalia, no campus exterior, no smiling-at-lens portrait. New constant: **the chair is always a real, used object** (worn foam, a tag, tape, a wobble), never a showroom chair. Tape (from idea 3) can be the label language across the system: masking tape with a role written in marker.

**The hierarchy of chairs (working typology):**

| Rung | Chair | What it says about the work |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | No chair: the floor, the line, the shift | You stand all day; standing is not chosen |
| 1 | The stool (bar, counter, lab, shift-lead) | Allowed to perch, not to settle |
| 2 | The folding chair (event, back of the room, reader's) | Temporary, stackable, put away after |
| 3 | The cubicle task chair (gas lift, worn arm pads) | Present, replaceable |
| 4 | The manager's chair (high back, the one with arms) | Decides |
| 5 | The director's chair (canvas, name on the back) | Authorship; the name is printed on it |

The design system tension Andres opened: in The Room she stands. So the strongest visual move is not "she climbs to a better chair" (status chasing) but "the chair she's been given is not where she's going to do her best work; she stands up." Standing on the red band is the rung above every chair. Note this flips "no chair" (rung 0, retail) into kinship: the person who's stood all shift already knows how to stand in front of a room.

## Direction 1. THE CHAIR SHE'S BEEN GIVEN / THE ROOM SHE STANDS IN

**Concept sentence:** on one side, the chair work gave her; on the other, the room she stands in front of; she's left the chair to stand on the red band.

**Card one (reads without copy?):** a split or diptych. Left: one worn chair, empty, in its real place (a stool behind a register, a task chair under fluorescent light). Right: the reverse-shot room, faces turned to the viewer, red band at the bottom. At thumb size it reads as "before / now" because the chair is empty and the room is full. Partial pass: the diptych grammar is familiar, so the chair has to be specific enough (tape, wear) to stop it reading as a furniture ad. Risk of reading as "then/now" glow-up.

**Tie to The Room and the band:** the right half is The Room unchanged; the red band runs under both halves, so it reads as one floor: she's walked off the left frame onto the band.

**Per program:**
- **MS:** the shift-lead stool beside a standing desk, or the cubicle task chair with someone else's name on a strip of tape → her applied-project presentation room with her invited colleagues in row two.
- **MFA:** the folding chair at the back of a reading (the listener's seat, an open-mic chair) → Get Lit! from the stage, front rows in spill light.
- **MOT:** the aide's chair she never sits in (a plastic chair in a hallway, unused, a clipboard on it) → Catalyst sim lab, cohort around the plinth.

**Carousel arc:** 1 the given chair, alone, tape label ("SHIFT LEAD", "ASSISTANT") · 2 the same chair, her hand lifting the bag off it (leaving) · 3 the reverse: the room watching you, red band · 4 the work, close, role-credited · 5 red end card, fact + request information.

**Single image:** the diptych with the band running under both halves; line on the band.

**Risk:** the before/after diptych is a category convention (and a weight-loss-ad grammar). Makes the old job look small, which may insult the job the prospect still needs to keep. Mitigation: the old chair is shown with respect (well lit, real), not shabby.

## Direction 2. THE TYPOLOGY (Becher grid)

**Concept sentence:** a grid of the chairs work gives people, photographed like industrial typologies, and one square in the grid holds no chair, just a red floor band with two footprints.

**Card one (reads without copy?):** a 3x3 grid of chairs, same angle, same flat grey light, same scale, black and white (Becher-style). Eight chairs: stool, folding chair, task chair, manager's chair, director's chair, a checkout perch, a break-room chair, a waiting-room chair. The ninth square: red band, empty floor, worn shoe marks. At thumb size the grid pattern plus one red break is very strong (a pattern with one exception is a cue the eye can't ignore). Reads as "one of these is not a chair" without copy. What it means needs card 2.

**Tie to The Room and the band:** the band is the one colour in a monochrome grid; it becomes the system's ownable mark without being a stamp (which A. The Red Mark failed). Card 2 or 3 opens the red square into The Room.

**Per program:** each program gets its own grid, the chairs from that field.
- **MS:** retail perch, shift-lead stool, call-center chair, cubicle task chair, assistant manager's chair, the regional manager's chair, boardroom chair, a hotel front-desk stool.
- **MFA:** the barista stool, the folding reader's chair, the library carrel chair, the slush-pile reader's desk chair, the editor's chair, the workshop seminar chair, the bar stool at an open mic, the bed edge where the writing actually happens.
- **MOT:** the CNA's (no chair, rendered as an empty corridor square in grey to separate it from the red), the wheelchair she pushes but never sits in (careful: see risk), the rehab-gym bench, the plastic family chair in a patient room, the therapist's rolling stool, the supervisor's desk chair.

**Carousel arc:** 1 the grid, one red square · 2 zoom: the chair she's in now, with its tape label · 3 the red square opens into The Room, watchers looking at you · 4 the work, credited by role · 5 fact + request information.

**Single image:** the grid itself; line on the band of the red square. Strong for OOH-style statics and the guide cover (a typology reads like a book cover).

**Risk:** the most "art school" of the five; could read as a design exhibit, cold, with no person. Becher reference is a style, not an idea, so it needs the red exception to carry meaning. MOT: a wheelchair in a chair typology is dangerous (patient equipment, disability read as a rung); exclude it. Ranking chairs into a grid can read as a status ladder (see system risks).

## Direction 3. THE EMPTY CHAIR IN THE WATCHERS' ROW (idea 3, systematized)

**Concept sentence:** in the row of people watching her work, one seat is the chair from her old title, empty, with the title taped on; she's not in it, she's up there.

**Card one (reads without copy?):** The Room's reverse shot, front row of faces, and one seat in the row is wrong: a cash-register stool among auditorium seats, a cubicle task chair among folding chairs, tape across it with a title in marker. The mismatch reads at thumb size (one object out of place in a row). Ambiguous first beat ("who's missing?") then the faces' light resolves it: everyone's looking at you. Strongest cold-read candidate; closest to the Core Creative Idea; least new production (one prop per shoot).

**Tie to The Room and the band:** pure extension: the same shot, one prop. The band is still under the viewer's feet.

**Per program:**
- **MS:** a shift-lead stool in the row, tape: "NO MANAGEMENT EXPERIENCE" or her current title ("SHIFT LEAD"). Pairs with the RTB "no management experience required".
- **MFA:** the folding chair at the back of a reading, moved into the front row, tape: "AUDIENCE". She's not in the audience any more.
- **MOT:** a plain plastic chair, tape: "AIDE", or the old career's title for a career changer ("RETAIL", "ADMIN"). The aide never sat; the chair being empty is literally true in both rooms.

**Carousel arc:** (from round 1, refined) 1 the row with the taped seat · 2 the reverse: her hand, slide/page/gait belt, red band · 3 the room after, the tape peeled off and stuck to the floor · 4 the work, role-credited · 5 fact + request information.

**Single image:** card 1, line on the band.

**Risk:** the empty chair is a memorial / grief code in some cultures and in news photography (empty chair = someone died, someone absent at the table). The tape and the out-of-place chair type must steer it to "misplaced" not "mourned". "A seat at the table" cliché is adjacent here: we're specifically avoiding that line; she's not asking for a seat, she gave one up.

## Direction 4. NO CHAIR (standing as the credential)

**Concept sentence:** for people who've stood all shift, standing in front of a room isn't the hard part; they've already earned the floor.

**Card one (reads without copy?):** the feet. Low angle, worn work shoes (non-slip kitchen shoes, scrubs clogs, retail sneakers) on a floor: shop tile, hospital lino, a stockroom. A red band runs across the floor right in front of the toes. No chair in frame; the absence is the subject. At thumb size: "feet at a line" reads immediately; "work shoes" reads with good casting of shoes. That it means "about to step into a room" needs card 2. Partial pass.

**Alternate card one:** the room's reverse shot, but she has been offered a chair (someone's pulled one up behind her, visible at frame edge) and she's still standing. Subtler, harder at thumb.

**Tie to The Room and the band:** the band is literally the line between the shift floor and the room's floor. Card 2 is the same band, now in The Room. The floor band was always "where you stand"; this direction makes standing the hero.

**Per program:**
- **MS:** retail floor / restaurant line / warehouse; the shift lead who runs the floor without a desk. The RTB "no management experience required" lands hardest here: she's been managing on her feet.
- **MFA:** the barista or bookstore clerk who writes before and after the shift; standing at the counter with a notebook. Or reading standing at a mic, no lectern.
- **MOT:** the strongest program: the aide or tech who never sits; OT itself is a profession on its feet (the therapist beside the client at the plinth). Standing is continuous from the old job into the new one, which avoids putting the old job down.

**Carousel arc:** 1 work shoes at the red band, shift floor · 2 same shoes, same band, now the sim lab / stage / presentation room floor · 3 the reverse: the room watching you · 4 the work, role-credited · 5 fact + request information. A match cut (shoes, band, new floor) works as a 6-second video.

**Single image:** the shoes on the band, half shift-floor, half room-floor (the floor material changes at the band).

**Risk:** romanticizing exhausting low-wage standing work ("grind" aesthetics); feet shots are a running-shoe ad convention. Mitigation: respect, not heroism; real shoes, no slow motion. Andres flagged this as possibly the most powerful for MOT/retail/service, and the reason holds: it's the only direction where the prospect's current life is the qualification, not the thing to escape.

## Direction 5. THE CHAIR SHE LEAVES BEHIND

**Concept sentence:** the chair is still warm, pushed back from the desk, jacket off; she stood up to go do the work in front of people.

**Card one (reads without copy?):** a task chair or stool, just vacated: rolled back at an angle, still turning slightly, a lanyard on the seat, a cup half-full. The room around it is her current job (break room, nurses' station, retail back office). At thumb size it reads "someone just left", which is intriguing but not yet "for something better". Weakest cold read alone; works as a sequence.

**Tie to The Room and the band:** card 1 is the reverse of The Room: the place she left. The band could run along the floor out of frame, a line leading out the door. Card 2 is The Room. This direction gives the system a "before" scene without a before/after diptych.

**Per program:**
- **MS:** the shift-lead stool at the counter, apron hung on it.
- **MFA:** the reader's folding chair at the back of the reading, notebook left on it, she's walked to the mic. (The listener's chair vs the reader's mic stand; the editor's chair at Willow Springs is a later card, credited by role.)
- **MOT:** the aide's hallway chair, gloves box on it, never sat in, and she's walked to the plinth.

**Carousel arc:** 1 the just-vacated chair · 2 the red band leading out of frame / the door · 3 the reverse: the room watching you · 4 the work, role-credited · 5 fact + request information. Also strong in motion: chair still rotating, cut to the room.

**Single image:** the vacated chair with the band running away from it along the floor.

**Risk:** "quit your job" is a dangerous implied promise for working adults who need to keep it (most of these programs are taken while working); legally and emotionally it should read as "stood up", not "walked out". Lower thumb read.

## Direction 6. TAKE YOUR SEAT (Andres's build, 2026-10-26)

Andres's input, relayed by the coordinator: he likes "Take a seat" ("Take your seat at the table." / "Your seat is waiting for you.").

**Concept sentence:** after she stands and shows her work, the room makes space for her: an empty chair, pulled back, at the table where the profession actually works.

**How it completes the arc:** The Room = standing and being watched (earning it). This direction = the seat that follows. It answers the hierarchy problem differently from 1–5: the seat isn't a rung on the ladder of titles; it's a working place among peers at one specific table. Stand first, then sit with them.

**Card one (reads without copy?):** a real table, mid-work (papers, laptops, manuscripts, a clipboard), people seated around it, one chair pulled back and angled toward the camera, empty, ready. The seated people look up at the camera position, the way people look up when someone walks in and they've been expecting her. At thumb size: "table + people + one pulled-out chair + eyes on me" reads as "that seat is for you". Probably a pass, and the most immediately warm card in the system. The weakness is the same reason it reads fast: it is the most familiar image here. Its ownability depends completely on the table being EWU's and unmistakably that program's.

**Tie to The Room and the band:** same camera (standing eye height, where she stands), same casting rule (peers outnumber faculty, faculty never the jury). The red band moves: it runs across the floor and stops at the chair legs, so the place you stand leads straight to the seat. Card one can also be read as the last frame of The Room.

**The table, per program (specific, never a boardroom):**
- **MS:** after the applied-project presentation, the working table where the cohort and her invited colleagues debrief, her slides still on the screen behind. Not a polished conference table: a seminar table with coffee cups and marked-up handouts. (Exact EWU room to confirm; this is the program where boardroom drift is strongest, so the props must say "cohort working session", not "executive meeting".)
- **MFA:** the Willow Springs editors' table, submissions in stacks, pencil notes, student editors around it; her chair pulled back with a manuscript waiting at that place. Strongest ownability (named, real, cleared imagery).
- **MOT:** the care-team huddle in the Catalyst sim lab: a standing-height counter or rolling table by the plinth, classmates and an instructor reviewing a plan, a gap left with a stool pulled out. Honest note: OT huddles are often standing, so the "seat" may be a stool or simply a gap in the circle; let the real room decide, don't force a chair.

**Carousel arc:** 1 the room watching you (The Room, red band) · 2 your work, close, credited by role · 3 the table, one chair pulled back, eyes on it · 4 the seat, close: her notebook/badge/manuscript already at the place (cohort membership, never a nameplate) · 5 red end card: fact + request information + the honest admission language.

**Single image:** card 3, the table with the pulled-out chair; the band leading to it carries the line.

**Risks (specific to this direction):**
- **Implied guaranteed admission.** "Your seat is waiting for you" reads as a promise of a place; cohorts are limited (MOT 32). Visual mitigations: no name card or nameplate on the chair; the object at the seat is the work (a manuscript, a plan), not her identity; the chair is shown after card 1–2 so it reads as earned by work, not handed out. Verbal: WordSmither to weigh "Your seat is waiting" against an earned form ("Earn your seat at the table" type, or "There's a seat for people who do the work"); end card carries apply/request language, never "reserve". Flag for clearances.
- **"A seat at the table" cliché.** It is the cliché named in the system risks below. Andres has chosen to use it deliberately; the way to own it is the specificity of the table (Willow Springs stacks, Catalyst plinth, cohort debrief) and the sequence (stand first). If the comp looks like any boardroom, it has failed.
- **Contradiction with 1–5.** 1–5 say "she leaves the chair and stands"; 6 says "she takes a seat". They are compatible as one arc (leave the given chair, stand in the room, take a seat among peers) but a single execution should pick one end point. Possible system: the old chair (taped title) → standing on the band → the new seat at the program's table.
- **Status reading** is lower here than in a title ladder, because the seat is among peers, not at the head of the table. Never the head chair.

## System risks (all directions)

- **Hierarchy reading as status-chasing.** A ladder of chairs naturally suggests "get the director's chair": a promotion promise the brief can't make and which clearances keep out (no promotion claimed or implied). The system protects itself by putting standing on the red band, not the top chair, as the destination. Never show the manager's or director's chair as the reward.
- **"A seat at the table" cliché.** It sits directly next to this territory; any line or image of her being offered or pulling up a chair at a conference table falls into it. In Directions 1–5, avoid the conference table entirely; their move is the opposite: she gives up a seat. Direction 6 uses the phrase on purpose (Andres's call) and owns it only through the specific program table.
- **Old job as lesser.** Every direction must keep the current chair dignified; prospects are still in it.
- **Empty chair = absence/grief** (Direction 3) and **feet = sneaker ad** (Direction 4): handled by specificity (tape, mismatched chair type; real worn work shoes).
- **MOT clinical care:** no wheelchair or patient equipment as a "rung"; no patients; client roles by classmates, per The Room rules.
- **Director's chair** is film coded; useful only for MFA if at all (name printed on the back = authorship), and even there it risks Hollywood cliché. Probably leave it out of the visuals and keep it as a typology entry.

## Combining (for the CD conversation, not a recommendation)

- Directions 3 and 4 combine cleanly: 3 is the Room shot with the taped old chair in the row; 4 is the "no chair" variant for programs whose prospects stood all shift (the taped object becomes a pair of work shoes or a floor tile, or the row has a standing gap). This could make "no chair" the MOT/MS-retail version and the taped chair the office/MFA version inside one system.
- Direction 2 (typology) can serve the long formats (guide cover, hub section) while 3/4 run social, sharing the tape-label language and the red band.
- Tape labels written in marker give WordSmither a native copy surface inside the image (titles, "MORE EXPERIENCE", "NOT YET"), consistent with round 1's promotion phrases.

Line hooks for WordSmither, placeholders only: "You've been standing all shift. Try standing here." · "Leave the stool." · "The chair was never the job."
