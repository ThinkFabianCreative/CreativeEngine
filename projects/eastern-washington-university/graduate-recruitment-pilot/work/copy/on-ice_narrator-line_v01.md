# On Ice: narrator line v01

CLIENT: Eastern Washington University · PROJECT: graduate-recruitment-pilot · SOURCE SCOPE: project-level
OBJECT: SCRIPT / VO line (narrator, at the reveal) · MODE: DEVELOP · Author: word-smither · 2026-11-08
Window: ~4s over near-silence (15–19.5s), calm, warm. Must hand off to her "Or not." Rules: `work/brief/creative-brief_v20.md` (lines 41, 60–61, 88).

## Baseline (Andres)

**"For many reasons, you've put your career on ice for way too long."** (13 words, about 4s calm; tight)

What works:
- "On ice" names the image at the exact moment we see it. Strong.
- "You've put" gives her the agency. That is right for "Stop waiting to be chosen": whoever put it on ice can take it off. It does not blame a boss or employer, so it passes the no-villain rule.
- "For many reasons" is generous. It concedes the reasons were real, so she is not a fool.

What to watch:
- **"Way too long" is the scold.** The blame risk is not in "you've put", it's here. It makes the warm narrator the last condescending voice in a film full of them, and it judges her. Brief line 41: she "does not quit, resent anyone or prove anyone wrong"; line 88: nobody looks like the villain. Calm narrator plus "way too long" leans that way.
- **The "Or not." handoff is soft.** "Or not" needs a proposal to refuse. After a statement of fact ("you've put it on ice too long"), "Or not." reads as "or I haven't", which is ambiguous. It snaps when the narrator offers to keep things the way they are.
- "For many reasons" is a little loose at 4s; it spends a beat.

## Alternatives (max 3)

**A. "For good reasons, you put your career on ice. It can stay there."** (13 words, about 4s)
Keeps Andres's structure and agency. "Good reasons" respects her; "It can stay there" is the warm, reasonable voice of the waiting culture, the most tempting voice in the film, and gives "Or not." something to refuse. Best handoff.

**B. "You put your career on ice. You can leave it there."** (11 words, about 3.5s)
The shortest. Room to breathe in the silence. Loses the "reasons" grace, so it sits colder; depends on a warm read.

**C. "There's always a good reason to keep your career on ice."** (11 words, about 3.5s)
Moves the waiting into the culture ("always a reason") rather than onto her. Cleanest on no-blame, but drops "you put", so less of the "you can take it off" logic. "Or not." works.

## Checks (A, recommended)

- No boss or employer: pass. No villain: the narrator sounds kind; the culture of waiting speaks through kindness. Pass.
- Not shaming: "good reasons" validates her past; no "too long". Pass.
- No outcome promise: says nothing about jobs or results. Pass.
- Nobody chooses her: the narrator does not invite or admit her; she refuses on her own. Pass.
- Handoff: "It can stay there." / "Or not." / break. Clean.
