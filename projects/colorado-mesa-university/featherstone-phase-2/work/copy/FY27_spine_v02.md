The spine is the idea in one read, for a jury or a university president. It is not a tagline. "Looking for something real?" stays the end line, unchanged.

What changed from v01: nobody cheats. Both teams play fair. The difference is what each side needs from the result. Featherstone lives on how it looks, so it has to win. CMU doesn't need anyone's approval, so a loss doesn't define it. Featherstone is the answer to Marshall's question: it's who we don't want to be.

## (a) Spine candidates

1. Featherstone needs to win. CMU just wants to play. Watch the difference, then decide who you want to be.
2. A school that can't afford to lose challenges a school that doesn't need to win, and students see which one they want.
3. Featherstone is the answer to "What don't you want?" The game shows students what they do want.
4. CMU plays a fair game against a school that lives for applause, and wins or loses like itself.
5. Two schools, one fair game: one needs the scoreboard to know who it is, the other already knows.
6. A fake Ivy that has to win takes on a real school that doesn't, and shows students another way to want it.
7. CMU takes Featherstone's challenge, plays it straight, and shows the kid nobody showed what wanting it honestly looks like.
8. We know who we don't want to be. So we're playing them.

## Pick

**A school that can't afford to lose challenges a school that doesn't need to win, and students see which one they want.** (#2, 20 words)

It puts the whole attitude gap in one sentence without anyone cheating or CMU claiming to be better, and it holds whatever the score. It ends on the student's choice, which is Marshall's question turned into a game.

Notes: #8 is the strongest line in the set but it's a headline, not a spine; a president needs to hear who "them" is. Keep it for the trailer or the challenge letter. #3 is the clearest bridge to the client's own premise and is a good backup if Marshall's question needs to be on the slide.

## (b) Strategy-line candidates

1. Show students, in public, the difference between needing to win and wanting to.
2. Make Featherstone the answer to "What don't you want?" so CMU can show what they do want.
3. Turn a fair game into proof that CMU students are free to be themselves, win or lose.
4. Give the student who wants it, and was never shown how, a side to pick.
5. Prove that at CMU, a scoreboard doesn't decide who you are.

## Pick

**Make Featherstone the answer to "What don't you want?" so CMU can show what they do want.** (#2)

It ties the client's premise and The Challenge into one campaign, which is the job of a brief slide. The "how" and the student insight live under it in the brief, where #4 works as the audience line.

## Satire check

Every candidate targets behavior: needing to win, living for approval, image over substance. None makes money, donors or success the joke, none codes Featherstone left or right, and none says CMU is better than the Ivies.
