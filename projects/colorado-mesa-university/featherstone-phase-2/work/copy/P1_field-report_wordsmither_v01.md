## 1. The campaign on one page

**POST-IT:** F.U.'s enrollment is falling, so its Headmistress goes undercover at CMU to find out what CMU has and take it. Everything she finds is who CMU is, and none of it will transfer.

**CAMPAIGN RULE:** Wherever CMU people are doing something they know, she circles it, calls it a threat and tries to take it. Every scheme runs on who she knows, and every one fails on what they know.

**THE LINE:** We care about what you know, not who you know. (S6, exact wording; client approval pending, Q18.)

**The truth underneath.** You can spy on someone's connections. You can't take what they know. It only exists in the person who learned it, and they'll teach it to you for free, which is the one price she can't pay.

### The genre deal: who is who

The user's references all share one structure. The establishment owns the club, the course and the rules. The underdogs are louder, rougher and self-made, and they win because they are actually better at the thing, usually by a technique the establishment sneered at.

| The film | Establishment | Underdog | How the underdog wins |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Animal House* | The polished fraternity; the dean | The loud, wild house | They are more alive than the rules, and the parade proves it |
| *Caddyshack* | The country club owner and his board | The down-to-earth golfer | He can play; the club melts down around its own rules |
| *Happy Gilmore* | The established rich golfer | The hockey player | A drive learned somewhere the establishment dismisses |
| **Field Report** | **F.U.: inherited, polished, clubby** | **CMU: sleeves rolled, loud, self-made** | **What they know. It was learned the long way, and it can't be bought, blocked or inherited** |

**Casting the establishment.**
- **The Headmistress** is Smails, Shooter and the dean in one. She is the club's enforcer, the smug rival and the bureaucrat who invents probation. She is excellent at her own game. See §4.
- **Dr. Augustus Sinclair Featherstone III** is the portrait on the clubhouse wall come to life. He runs the board and melts down on cue.
- **Thaddeus Featherstone IV** is the heir who has everything and knows nothing yet. He's sympathetic, never stupid: the joke is on the transfer, not on him.

**Casting the underdog.** CMU is an ensemble, and blue collar is its attitude, not its job list. The nursing student at 6 a.m., the researcher, the performer, the welder, the kid running the TV studio: sleeves rolled, loud when something works, unimpressed by surnames. The ensemble always covers several fields [T04] and is never only trades. **Nobody at CMU ever serves F.U.** No one fixes her car, carries her bags or waits her table. When they help her, it is teaching, from above: the underdogs outrank.

**What "wild" means here.** Noise, joy, crowds, a room that erupts when a demo works, the parade. There is no alcohol, hazing, voyeurism or caricature. Prospective students are the audience.

**Genre set pieces, borrowed as logic, never as lines or music:**
- **The unorthodox-technique win (*Happy Gilmore*).** In the Showcase blackout, the presenter gives her talk from memory, walking the room the way she learned to give reasons in a county-fair judging ring at nine. A technique the club would sneer at wins the room. The epilogue super names where she learned it.
- **The establishment meltdown (*Caddyshack*).** F.U.'s boardroom hears her report and falls apart: a portrait, an easel, a candelabra, then the sprinklers.
- **The parade and the epilogue (*Animal House*).** After the Showcase, CMU spills out loud into golden-hour light, carrying its projects, and carries the topiary along like a float. Freeze frames with yellow supers tell us what each of them knows and what became of the Headmistress.

- **The Headmistress connects them without anyone explaining.** "They call themselves Mavericks, sir. Unbranded." A maverick is an unbranded animal, one you can't trace to an owner. **Word count:** about 170 spoken words in 90 seconds, so beats 3, 6 and 8 have room to breathe. That room is where CMU's screen time lives.

**Why each beat is there.**
- **Beat 3 is CMU's portrait.** Four fields, one attitude. Her notes warm up while she tries not to let them.
- **Beat 4 is A's law in one image.** She acquires the helmet and not the welding. The welder's offer is the generosity response, and her horror at "free" is the laugh.
- **Beat 5 is the fixed turn.** In round 1 she softened here. Now the moment of being asked what she thinks is the most dangerous thing that has happened to her. She eats the evidence and states F.U.'s creed out loud ("I don't know things. I know *people*."), which is the who-you-know side of the line in the villain's own words. The snap-back also motivates the climax: she cuts the power *because* they got to her. The antagonist leaves this beat stronger.
- **Beat 6 is the spine and the proof.** Take away the tools, and what they know keeps going. The phone lights are the ensemble answering, so no one needs to make a speech. The presenter's walk-the-aisle delivery is the *Happy Gilmore* technique, and the epilogue pays off where she learned it.
- **Beat 7 is the *Caddyshack* meltdown,** and it names CMU through the enemy's mouth.
- **Beat 8 is the *Animal House* epilogue.** CMU literally carries her, which is the underdogs outranking. The supers must never state real outcomes (jobs, salaries, graduation) without approval.

**Brand-swap test.** Put another logo on the end card and "Unbranded" stops making sense, so the film belongs to the Mavericks. Without the Maverick line, the film belongs to CMU only as far as the [T] scenes are real. That is the honest dependency (§9).

**Risks.** (1) Density. Nine beats, so the edit has to keep F.U. scenes short and give CMU scenes time (DesignChamp §1.5). (2) Eating the worksheet can play as gross or as delight. Keep it quick and dignified, as if it is normal protocol. (3) The phone lights must read as an audience helping, not filming the presenter.

### 3.1 End card: the line, "Maverick at Heart" and the 8-word guidance (Q30)

**The problem.** The line is 10 words. S12 says: "We recommend using this high contrast dynamic styling on headlines of 8 words or less." The headline lockup is three fonts, no more than three rows.

**Proposal: split the two jobs across two beats and two type treatments.** DesignChamp owns the final setting.

- **Beat (a), the line: one voice, not the high-contrast lockup.** Set it in a single style, PF Grand Gothik Wide Bold (the S12 H2 treatment), in three rows, centered on Mavroon (graphic background, so centered per S12 layout):
  ```
  WE CARE ABOUT
  WHAT YOU KNOW,
  NOT WHO YOU KNOW.
  ```
  This stays inside the recommendation because the high-contrast styling isn't used, and it respects the three-row limit. The line is an argument, so it should read at an even pace, like a sentence. Mixed fonts would make "WHAT" and "WHO" compete.
- **Beat (b), the signature: the high-contrast lockup goes to MAVERICK AT HEART.** Three words, well inside the guidance. The CMU horizontal signature sits with it and gets clear space. One branding mark per piece.
- **Why this order works.** The line closes the story. MAVERICK AT HEART lands last and loudest, as the payoff to "Unbranded". The brand's most energetic type treatment falls on the brand's own words.
- **Short formats (:06, OOH, 4:5 social).** Where there is room for only one frame, the line sits bottom-centered over photography in the single style, with the signature and tagline below. Never break the line across two pieces or cards (it's one sentence, and its wording is mandatory).
- **A request to put to the client, not our default:** if CMU prefers the line in the three-font lockup, set "WHAT YOU KNOW," in the heavy weight and the other two rows in Condensed Book. The contrast then falls on three words. Ask whether the 8-word recommendation flexes for this. Default to the compliant version until the answer comes.
- **Open:** whether the tagline appears with the logo as a lockup S12 already defines, or as separate type (DesignChamp to check S12; C12 covers the Maverick name).

## 4. The Headmistress: voice bible

### Character note

She is the establishment's best player. In F.U.'s world her tools never fail: a phone call to someone's godfather, the seating chart, the right surname dropped at the right moment, a stare that reorganizes a room. She knows things too. She knows precedence, protocol, leverage, and whose mother sits on which board. Everything she knows is about *who*. That's her tragedy and the campaign's argument.

She is Smails's pomposity, Shooter's smugness and the dean's bureaucratic menace, played with total conviction. She's never a buffoon. Her disguises are bad because F.U. has never needed to blend in, not because she is stupid. Her camouflage is new and has the tags on; it has no wear.

Her comic engine is self-correction under pressure: "Efficient. I meant efficient." (Phase 1) becomes "Impressive. I meant suspicious." Each time CMU gets to her, she snaps back harder, with more paperwork, more escalation and colder composure. She never learns her lesson on screen. The audience learns it for her.

**Register:** clipped, formal, condescending, with "dear" as a weapon. She speaks in sentences made for minutes and memoranda. She whispers into a dictaphone like a field agent and speaks to the board like a butler with a knife.

**Hard rules (kept from round 1):** she spies on tasks, objects and places of work, never on young people's bodies or living spaces. No residence halls. The circle lands on the task, never the person. Everyone she watches is an adult at work.

## 8. Selling the work: three objections and the answers

These are the objections I'd expect in the room, with the defense for each. The director owns the presentation and the selling-the-work framework; this is the copy side of the defense.

### Objection 1: "Are you making us the slobs? The blue-collar school? The party school?"

**What's behind it:** fear that the genre reframes CMU as less serious, or narrows it to trades.

**Answer:**
- **In every one of those films, the underdog wins because they're better at the thing.** That's the only part we borrow. The loud, sleeves-rolled energy is how CMU *feels*; what CMU *does* is win on knowledge, every time, on screen.
- **Blue collar is an attitude, not a job category.** The ensemble includes a researcher, a nurse, a performer, a broadcaster and a welder, and the presenter who wins the Showcase is a researcher. Nobody is cast as F.U.'s help; CMU people only ever teach her.
- **This is CMU's own brand, played at volume.** S12 asks for Down to Earth, Playful and Daring, and describes Resourceful as "finding creative solutions even in places where there appear to be none". That's the blackout beat, word for word.
- **There is no party material.** No alcohol, hazing or chaos for its own sake. The "wild" is a room erupting when something works.
- **What must not be traded away:** the loud joy. Sand it down to polite competence and CMU becomes F.U. with a mesa.

### Objection 2: "Why is the villain on screen so much? Phase 2 was supposed to be about us."

**What's behind it:** S10, "this phase should demonstrate who we are", and a worry that the Headmistress is the star.

**Answer:**
- **Her camera is CMU's camera.** She can't spy without showing us CMU, so every surveillance frame is CMU footage. In the :30, about two-thirds of the running time is CMU people at work.
- **She's the most credible narrator CMU could have.** She's trying to find something wrong, and every report says the opposite. CMU never has to make a claim about itself; its enemy does it by accident.
- **The always-on system is mostly CMU.** The Epilogue series, Where's the Headmistress? and the dossier are CMU people, places and skills, with her as the frame.
- **The honest condition:** this only works with real CMU truths. The [T] list in §9 is what decides whether this is a portrait of CMU or a well-made spoof. We need the client's help with the list more than their approval of the jokes.

### Objection 3: "Surveillance of students, satire of higher ed: is this safe for us?"

**What's behind it:** consent, creepiness with a young audience, legal exposure, political heat, and the timing of CMU's gift.

**Answer:**
- **Hard rules are built in.** She watches tasks and places of work, never bodies or living spaces. No residence halls. The circle lands on the task. Everyone she watches is an adult at work, cast and released [T04]. The dossier is clearly fiction and clearly CMU's.
- **Only F.U. is satirized, and F.U. is fictional.** No real institution or person is named or implied. Her targets are legacy, favors and surnames, never donors or giving, which also keeps us clear of CMU's recent gift (Q28).
- **No CMU claim rides on the joke.** F.U.'s falling enrollment is F.U.'s own fiction, and blaming CMU is its paranoia. No numbers, charts or outcomes about CMU. The line is the only argument CMU makes.
- **What we need from CMU:** legal review of the satire (Q8), the license decision for F.U. accounts naming CMU (Q31), the Maverick decision, and releases.

**The load-bearing elements to defend in any round of cuts:** the blackout beat; "Impressive. I meant suspicious."; the generosity response; the snap-back after the classroom turn; real CMU people in the epilogue. Everything else can flex.
