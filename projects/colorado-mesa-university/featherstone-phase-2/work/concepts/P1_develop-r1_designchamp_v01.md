## 0. What holds across all three directions

### 0.1 The wear code, developed

Rule: **privilege looks brand new; knowing leaves marks.** F.U. owns the untouched object. CMU owns the used one. Wear is CMU's visual evidence, not its premise, and it runs through all three directions.

The guardrail is that wear must read as **mastery, never hardship**. These six rules make it hold:

1. **Wear is local.** It appears only at the point of contact, where a hand repeats: the thumb groove on a tool handle, the keys worn blank on a calculator, the spine cracked at the chapter that mattered, graphite shine on the side of a hand. The rest of the object is sound. General decay, peeling, rust-through or broken things are out.
2. **Worn bright before worn dark.** Prefer burnish, polish and smoothing (brass rubbed gold by hands, a wooden handle glossed by grip, ivory keys yellowed at middle C). Dirt appears only as fresh working residue from today: chalk, sawdust, graphite, flour. It is never grime from neglect.
3. **Wear sits on instruments, not on lives.** It belongs on tools, books, instruments and equipment, never on clothing, homes or bodies. No holes in jeans, no patched coats, no shabby kitchens. The student's flannel is clean.
4. **Repair only by choice.** A handle re-wrapped in tape to fit one hand reads as expertise. A cracked screen held together with tape reads as poverty. Keep the first and cut the second (this drops the "taped calculator" from my EXPLORE round).
5. **Light wear like treasure.** Worn objects get the same care F.U. gives its heirlooms: controlled key light, a macro lens, a raking side light that shows texture as relief. No desaturated grunge grade. Wear is photographed, never applied as a texture overlay or a distressed filter.
6. **Wear is present tense.** A hand is in frame, or has just left it. Wear belongs to a person who is still using the thing, not to a relic.

F.U.'s inverse code: **tags still attached, fold creases from the packet, protective film, shrink-wrap, vitrines, symmetry, nothing ever touched.** Its gilt is foil and never burnished. Its books have unbroken spines.

### 0.2 CMU's brand colors and the wear code

The Phase 1 end card (spot2_04): a maroon field, overlapping orange-to-yellow arcs, the white MCU monogram and COLORADO MESA UNIVERSITY wordmark, COLORADOMESA.EDU. Phase 1 has already solved half the problem without saying so: the student wears a **maroon plaid flannel with mustard-yellow backpack straps**. CMU's palette already lives on someone who uses things.

Resolution:

| Where | Rule |
|---|---|
| **In the world (scenes, photography)** | CMU's colors appear only on things in use: the flannel, a backpack strap, the maroon paint worn off a tool handle, a yellow pencil down to its last third, orange hi-vis on a work glove. They are never graphic fields laid on photography. |
| **Proportion in a CMU frame** | ~70% natural material neutrals (brick, timber, graphite, denim, steel, paper), ~20% maroon, at most ~10% yellow/orange, placed at the point of use so the color does the "direct" job (visual-craft §4). |
| **Proportion in an F.U. frame** | ~70% cream and pastel (powder blue, blush), ~20% gilt and dark wood, ~10% the Headmistress's teal and pink. No maroon, orange or yellow ever. |
| **The end card** | Untouched, as supplied. It is the one clean graphic surface on CMU's side, and it earns that by arriving last as a signature, not as a scene. Transition: match-cut from the last CMU color in the scene (maroon of a worn handle, yellow of the strap) straight into the maroon field. |
| **Prohibited** | Distressing, scuffing or texturing the CMU logo or arcs. Pastel or gilt treatments of CMU identity. CMU's logo on any F.U. surface. F.U.'s crest on any CMU surface. |

Typography follows the same split. **F.U.** gets an engraved, high-contrast serif in letterspaced caps, gilt foil, and copperplate script for heirs' names (continuous with the Phase 1 microsite). **CMU** gets its identity typography, set clean and in sentence case. The campaign sans is still to be supplied: Phase 1 social and DOOH used a heavy italic grotesque, but no approved spec has been seen (flagged). **The line is always set in CMU's type, on CMU's side of the frame, and never distressed.** No handwriting fonts: handwriting appears only as real marks photographed on real objects.

### 0.3 The Headmistress as a visual object

She is the Phase 1 tour guide: teal plaid suit, pink ruffled blouse, pearls, clipboard. In Phase 2 she is **an F.U. object dropped into CMU's world**, and three constants make the contrast read in one frame:

1. **She is the only pastel in a CMU frame**, at 10–15% of the frame, which makes her the focal intrusion. Everything around her follows the CMU proportion above.
2. **She brings her own light.** When she's on CMU ground, she is lit by an F.U. key: soft, frontal, faintly pink, with no shadow. That light doesn't match the location's daylight or practicals. You can see a warm pink spill on the wall behind her that nothing in the room explains. She looks composited into reality. This is the cheapest and most ownable gag in the system, and it works in stills, film and DOOH.
3. **Her kit is pristine and still has its tags on.** Opera-glass binoculars in mother-of-pearl on a pearl chain. A crest-embossed dossier in blush leather. A gilt-capped fountain pen. A disguise that fools no one because it is brand new: a hi-vis vest with fold creases from the packet, a hard hat with the sticker still on, a hoodie with the price tag swinging. **Her disguise fails because it has no wear.** That is the wear code working as comedy: she can buy the costume but not the marks.

Director's rule held: every scheme fails **because of what someone knows**, and CMU barely notices her. The comic geometry is **depth staging**. She's sharp in the foreground, mid-scheme. Behind her, in focus, the scheme has already been quietly undone by someone doing something competently who never looks at her.

C12 applies to every Headmistress scene on CMU ground. The default is **CMU-real but unnamed**: generic campus textures (brick, study rooms, workshops, quads) with no signage, no mascot, no identifiable buildings and no named events. The Phase 1 precedent (University Center exterior, mascot) is flagged, not assumed.

## A. NON-TRANSFERABLE

### A1. Key visual

**Concept sentence.** The Featherstone estate is inventoried for the heirs: every item is pristine and tagged with a name. At the end of the table sits the one thing that can't be passed on, and it is the only object in the room that shows use.

**What carries the idea.** Visual-verbal. The picture shows the rule and the tags do the joke. The line only confirms it.

**Layout (horizontal, 3:2 master).** A long mahogany library table seen from a low three-quarter angle, running from the left edge to the right third. Along it, in F.U. lighting (soft top light, no shadows), sit the inheritances. Each one is mint and wears a gilt-edged cream tag on string with an heir's name in copperplate:
a scale model of a Newport-style house under a glass dome; a board chair with a velvet seat; an engraved brass surname plate; a model yacht; a sealed crest envelope stamped LEGACY ADMISSION.
At the far right end, in the same careful light, sits one worn object: a calculus notebook with a broken spine, page edges greyed by graphite, a pencil worn to a stub lying across it. Its tag is identical, but where the heir's name should be it carries a red rubber stamp: **NON-TRANSFERABLE**.

**Reading order.**
1. The gilt-and-pastel row with its tags (premise: this is an inheritance).
2. The one used object at the end of the row (anomaly: why is this here?).
3. The red NON-TRANSFERABLE stamp (turn: it can't be passed on).
4. The line, lower right under the notebook, in CMU type.
5. The CMU lockup on a maroon band along the bottom edge (who it's from).

The notebook sits on the right third so the eye travels the whole inheritance before it arrives. It must not be larger than the house under the dome, or the reveal arrives before the setup (Barry §5).

### A2. Visual system

| | F.U. | CMU |
|---|---|---|
| Objects | Heirlooms, deeds, keys, signet rings, crests: mint, tagged, under glass | The one used object per execution: a notebook, a torque wrench, a violin bow with its hair worn, a stethoscope's worn bell. Always an instrument of knowing |
| Light | Even top light, museum reverence | The same light, which is the point: the worn object is treated as the heirloom |
| Type | Engraved caps for inventory headings; copperplate for heirs' names | Line in CMU type; the stamp is the only red |

**Signature device: the estate tag.** It is a cream tag with a gilt edge on a string. On F.U. objects it names the heir. On the worn object it is stamped NON-TRANSFERABLE, or in motion it simply won't stay tied (see A4, DOOH). It scales from a favicon (the tag silhouette) to a 3D object hung on a physical billboard. The generative rule for new executions: **tag something that can be inherited, then try to tag something that can't.**

### A3. The Headmistress still: "The Collector"

A CMU study room at night, real but unnamed. There are rolling whiteboards dense with working-out, worn chairs and a radiator. A student (back three-quarters to camera) is mid-problem at the board, chalk dust on her flannel cuff and a sleeve pushed up. In the foreground left, crouched behind a stack of chairs, is the Headmistress in the teal plaid, lit by her own pink key, opera glasses raised on their pearl chain. In her other hand she holds a crystal specimen jar with a gilt lid and a crest label in copperplate: **"Specimen: Calculus. For Thaddeus."** The jar is empty and will stay empty.
One-frame read: pastel operative, empty jar, full whiteboard. The scheme fails because knowledge can't be collected, and the student never turns around.

### A6. Film look

- **F.U.:** locked-off tripod or slow motorized dolly on track, centered compositions, a clean modern lens set at 32–40 mm, soft top light with no shadows, cream and pastel grade with gilt highlights. Everything is in focus because nothing in it matters more than anything else.
- **CMU:** shoulder-mounted but steady (the camera breathes), longer lenses at 50–85 mm observing across space, older glass with some texture, available light plus practicals, true color. Shallow focus finds the point of contact: hands, keys, the chalk.
- **She on CMU ground:** she is always shot in F.U. grammar (locked, centered, her pink key) while everything around her stays handheld. The cut between her and the room is a cut between two films. The macro wear inserts use F.U.'s reverent light, so the only moment the two worlds share a look is when the camera honors a worn object.

### A7. What a 17-year-old sees

"Non-transferable" is printed on every concert ticket and gift card they've held. They know the nepo-baby conversation. And they'll recognize their own worn stuff: the calculator from ninth grade with its most-used keys faded, the controller with its thumbstick worn smooth, the sketchbook softened at the corner. They see their own marks treated as the valuable object in the room. They are not the heirs; they are the notebook.

### A8. Proof flags and risks

Flags: any study room, workshop or campus in the Headmistress still (C12); any programme implied by the worn object (e.g. a stethoscope implies nursing) [NEEDS APPROVED CMU PROOF]; president casting (Q24/Q19 adjacent); airport media rights; UGC with minors.

Top 3 risks:
1. **Clever, cold.** The inventory is a sharp diagram with no warmth. The fix is the hand in frame on every CMU object, and the student in the film.
2. **Generic visual cliché: knolling and the "authentic worn notebook."** Flat-lay grids of objects are the default "curated" layout, and a beaten notebook is stock-library "grit". Keep the inventory a real table in a real room at a real angle, not a top-down flat-lay. Rotate the worn instrument each time so the notebook doesn't become the logo.
3. **Wear reading as hardship** if the worn object looks old rather than used. Apply rule 2 (worn bright) and rule 6 (present tense) without exception.

## B. PROUDLY REJECTED

### B1. Key visual

**Concept sentence.** F.U. reads an application by blacking out everything you know and keeping only your surname. The rejection letter it sends back gets pinned up with pride, because every blacked-out line is proof of something you know.

**What carries the idea.** Visual-verbal, and behavioral in social. The bars carry the idea. The one bar with its corner lifted carries CMU's side.

**Layout (portrait, 4:5 master; re-composes to 9:16).** An F.U. rejection letter fills 80% of the frame. It is heavy cream laid paper with a blind-embossed crest and gilt border. The applicant's answers are quoted back in the letter, and every one is covered by a bar of **black foil**, not marker: glossy, crisp and expensive-looking. F.U. redacts in luxury. The bars are long and uneven, and their lengths alone suggest someone who knows a great deal. The only legible line is typed: **SURNAME: DELGADO — NOT RECOGNIZED**, with a blush-pink REJECTED stamp beside it. One bar, the fourth, has its corner **peeled back** like a sticker to show a few words of the answer beneath: [COPY: a specific, funny "what I know" answer]. The letter is not floating. It is held by a magnet on a worn fridge door, or by a strip of tape on a workshop pegboard among tools, with the surrounding objects barely in focus.

**Reading order.**
1. The black bars (stop: a lot has been hidden).
2. The one legible line, surname NOT RECOGNIZED (premise: F.U. only reads names).
3. The peeled corner and the words under it (turn: look what they threw out).
4. The fridge or pegboard context (resolution: this letter is displayed proudly).
5. The line in CMU type in the bottom margin, then the lockup.

### B2. Visual system

| | F.U. | CMU / the public |
|---|---|---|
| Objects | Application forms, rejection letters, envelopes with wax seals, black foil bars | The places letters end up: fridges, lockers, dashboards, pegboards, back pockets, all worn by use |
| Color | Cream, gilt, black foil, one blush stamp | Material neutrals; maroon and yellow only on the objects in use around the letter |
| Type | Engraved caps for the form; typewriter for the reviewer's line | The answers under the bars are written in the applicant's own hand or typed plainly, as real text, never a script font |

**Signature device: the redaction bar with one corner lifted.** F.U. puts the bar down; someone who knows lifts the corner. This is the metaphor only. It must never imply that CMU reads applications name-blind or treats legacy status in any particular way [NEEDS APPROVED CMU PROOF for any literal use]. The generative rule: **F.U. hides what someone knows; one corner shows it.**

### B3. The Headmistress still: "The Noticeboard"

Early morning in a real but unnamed CMU corridor. A huge public corkboard, layered with years of pins and flyers: "I'll fix your bike", "calc study group Tues", "band needs a drummer", hand-drawn, with tear-off tabs. The Headmistress stands on a small gilt step stool in the teal plaid, lit by her own pink key, redacting the board with a gold-capped black-foil pen, meticulous, pearls swinging. Every flyer she has reached is barred except its surname line.
The failure is in the details, and the photo editor catches it on a second look: **the tear-off tabs under her black bars are already gone.** People already took the numbers. The people who know things have already found each other. A student walks past in the background, tearing off the last tab from a flyer she hasn't reached yet, and doesn't glance up.
(Flyer content must stay generic and unnamed; no named CMU clubs or services, C12.)

### B5. PR press photograph: the public, proudly rejected

A horizontal telephoto frame down a long public queue in a city plaza. The long lens compresses it so it seems endless. The people are specific, not stock: a welder in coveralls, a nurse in scrubs, a grandmother, a teenager in a hoodie, a rancher. The repeating cream letters make the pattern, and the faces make the story. The caption writes itself: people queue to be turned away.
Casting, releases and the "queue" reading (it must look joyful, never needy) are flagged.

### B6. Film look

- **F.U. admissions panel:** candle and chandelier, long table, portraits, overhead top light, cool pastel shadows, locked-off symmetrical wides, slow push-ins on the panel's faces.
- **Applicants:** each is shot where they know things (a barn, a garage, a kitchen, a lab bench) as documentary portraits at eye level, 35–50 mm, window light, a steady handheld camera, true color. When they talk about what they know, the camera finds their hands.
- **Crossing the worlds:** the panel "interviews" applicants by video call. The applicant's world plays on a gilt-framed screen inside the F.U. room, the only real light in there. The panel leans toward it.
- **She on CMU ground:** she collects evidence with her own pink key and her own locked-off shots, while the corridor around her runs on documentary coverage.

### B7. What a 17-year-old sees

Application season, which is their own anxiety: essays, forms, "list your activities". The who-you-know questions satirize what they suspect other people's applications get to say. **Decision-reaction videos** are a genre they already watch and make, and this parodies it with a rejection they can celebrate. The peeled corner is theirs: a thing they know that no form asked about.

### B8. Proof flags and risks

Flags: the bar as a name-blind or legacy-policy claim [NEEDS APPROVED CMU PROOF]; anything framed as an admissions outcome; campus corridor and noticeboard (C12); minors' data (microsite, UGC, mailed letters); public submissions need consent and moderation; capitol-route media is UNVERIFIED.

Top 3 risks:
1. **Generic visual cliché: redaction.** Black bars carry true-crime, surveillance and leak-document codes and are a tired design trope. The luxury foil, the cream laid stock and the lifted corner have to make it read as F.U.'s stationery, not a government file. If the black-foil version still reads as "leak", the bar should become a gilt-foil bar.
2. **Admissions claim creep.** The whole direction sits closest to describing CMU's admissions. The CMU side must stay a celebration, never an application.
3. **Visually thinnest as a lone image.** A document is a weaker still than a person or a place. B lives in social and PR, and the OOH depends on the physical peel. Without the build, it is a form on a wall.

## C. RESERVED FOR LEGACIES

### C1. Key visual

**Concept sentence.** F.U. ropes off the public tools of knowing, a piano in a square, a chess table, a lookout telescope, a public chalkboard, "Reserved for Legacies". Wherever the hook hangs open, someone who actually knows how to use the thing has stepped in and is using it.

This is the fix the director asked for. The rope no longer surrounds just any amenity. It surrounds **instruments that are useless unless you know how to use them.** F.U. reserves them for people who won't use them, and the unclip is done by the one who can. So the rope's opening is about what you know, not only about access.

**What carries the idea.** Visual. Rope, hook and person in action. The line signs it.

**Layout (horizontal, 3:2 master).** A public piano in a small, sunlit town square, fictional or with permits, in no identifiable real town. Four polished brass stanchions surround it with **blush-pink velvet rope**, not club red: F.U.'s pastel carried out of the castle, which also avoids the nightclub code. On the front post is a small engraved brass plaque: **RESERVED FOR LEGACIES · FEATHERSTONE UNIVERSITY**. One rope end is unclipped, its brass snap hook hanging and swinging, the rope sagging to the paving. On the bench, a person in work clothes plays with hands in slight motion blur. The ivory is worn yellow around middle C: this piano gets played. Passers-by have stopped at the edges of the frame.

**Reading order.**
1. The pink rope and brass (stop: something is roped off).
2. The plaque (premise: reserved for legacies).
3. The hanging hook, in sharp focus at the frame's optical center (turn: someone opened it).
4. The person playing, hands blurred (resolution: the one who knows how).
5. The line in the upper-left sky or wall space in CMU type, then the lockup lower right.

### C2. Visual system

| | F.U. | CMU / the public |
|---|---|---|
| Objects | Brass stanchions, pink velvet rope, engraved plaques, ribbon-cutting scissors | The instruments inside the rope, worn bright at the point of use: piano keys, chess pieces rubbed pale, a telescope eyepiece, a chalk tray |
| Behavior | Places, polishes, guards | Unclips, then gets on with it. No confrontation, no speech |
| Sound | A ribbon-cutting fanfare | **The brass click of the snap hook**: the sonic signature, used as the first sound of every film and reel |

**Signature device: the unclipped hook.** A brass snap hook hangs from a pink rope. It scales from a favicon and profile mark (hook silhouette) to a physical build (real stanchions at a billboard base). It never replaces the CMU logo; it signs the campaign, and the logo signs the university. The generative rule: **F.U. ropes an instrument; someone who can use it opens it and uses it.**

Escalation engine (for film and social): after the hook fails, F.U. upgrades its closures, and each one is undone by someone who knows. A padlock is opened by someone who remembers the combination is the founder's birthday, printed on the plaque. An elaborate "Legacy Knot" is undone by a climber with one practiced pull. A patented Featherstone Clasp is opened by an engineering-minded kid in four seconds. (Keep every opening legal and non-destructive: no lock-picking, no cutting. The point is knowing, not breaking.)

### C3. The Headmistress still: "The Knot"

Dusk on a campus quad, real but unnamed (brick, lawn, no signage), where an outdoor chess table has been roped off in pink velvet. The Headmistress, in teal plaid with her own pink key, has replaced the hook with an elaborate, many-looped "Legacy Knot" in the rope and is peering from behind a hedge in the right foreground through mother-of-pearl opera glasses. In the center of the frame, in focus, a student with chalk on her hands and a climbing harness clipped to her backpack pulls one tail, and the whole knot falls open in a single loop (the frame catches the rope mid-release). She doesn't look toward the hedge. Behind her, two people are already sitting down to play.

### C8. Proof flags and risks

Flags: quad, chess table and any campus location (C12); a real town or park (permits, public-space law, local sensitivity, Q8); a real public piano program (don't imply partnership); talent releases for people in the press photograph.

Top 3 risks:
1. **Access drift.** Even with instruments inside the rope, the image can still read as "open to all" rather than "what you know". Every execution must show the thing in skilled use, never just a person walking through.
2. **Generic visual cliché: the velvet rope as nightclub or VIP code.** Red rope plus brass reads as a club door. The blush-pink rope, the engraved F.U. plaque and the everyday public setting must be non-negotiable.
3. **Public anger before the joke.** Roping real public goods, even briefly, can land as a real act of exclusion. Clip the hook open from the start in live installations wherever the rope would block use, and never rope anything accessibility-critical.

## Comparative note

**Visually strongest: C.** It has the best single image (rope, hook and a person in action read in two seconds at any size), the best press photograph, the only sonic mark, and a device that works as a physical object, a film insert and a favicon. With the rope moved onto instruments of knowing, it now carries the line rather than an access message. Its weakness is that wear enters only at the point of use inside the rope. It shows the code but doesn't argue it.

**A is the most complete system.** It is the only direction where wear is the argument itself (the one used object is the non-transferable one), and its inventory premise will generate executions indefinitely. It is colder than C, and its best image is a diagram. **B is the weakest visually on its own:** a document still, with a cliché to beat. It is the strongest behaviorally, and it lives in social and PR.

**Can one visual system hold all three? Yes, at the level of rules, but not at the level of devices.** A, B and C share four constants: the wear code (0.1), the color and brand-card split (0.2), the Headmistress as an F.U. object with her own light (0.3), and one structural rule, which is that **every F.U. device is an instrument of exclusion, and CMU's mark is that device undone by someone who knows** (the tag that won't tie, the bar with its corner lifted, the hook hanging open). That rule is a real family resemblance and passes the brand-blind test. Running all three devices at once would be a kitchen sink, though. Pick one lead device. My recommendation is to lead with **C's hook** as the campaign's mark and let A's estate tag and B's lifted bar appear as F.U. escalations inside the same world, not as competing signatures. If the director leads with A for the platform, the tag leads and the hook becomes a stunt.
