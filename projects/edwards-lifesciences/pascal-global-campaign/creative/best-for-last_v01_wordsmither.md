# Best for Last: line exploration v01 (WordSmither)

CLIENT: Edwards Lifesciences · PROJECT: PASCAL Global Campaign · MODE: DEVELOP · MLR off for the pitch.
Inputs: creative-brief_v03.md (J1 key takeout: our own PASCAL experience is the reason to use it every day; umbrella rule: never "every case" for PASCAL), review-cd_all-sketches_v05.md §6.

Insight (Andres): operators save PASCAL for the hard cases. It's what they reach for when they mean business. The job is to turn that trust into everyday use.

## 1. Honest read of Andres's two lines

**"Don't save the best for last."**
- Compression: good. Six short words, says the insight in one go.
- Surprise: low on its own. It's a stock idiom and a song title (Vanessa Williams). The reader finishes it before they finish reading it, and nothing turns at the end.
- What it gets right: it names the behaviour (saving it) without describing the hard case. Hard case stays as provenance, so it passes the brief rule.
- Risks: "best" is a superlative claim, which is fine with MLR off but will be the first thing cut later. "Last" can read as end of the procedure list, end of the treatment pathway, or a last resort for the patient. The last one is the sharp reading and also the risky one. It's an instruction, which talks down a little to senior operators. Peers don't usually tell each other "don't".
- Verdict: REWORK. Keep the idea of "saving it", lose the idiom as the headline. It works better as a spoken line inside body copy or as a sales/meeting line than on a poster.

**"Use it when it matters the most."**
- As written it describes the current habit and agrees with it. It tells them to keep doing what they're doing. Andres's own note is right.
- With the turn, "Use it when it matters most. It always does.", it becomes a proper reversal: the second line takes back the first. That is fresher than the idiom, because the setup is something the reader already believes and the turn comes from their own values (every patient matters).
- Risks: "It always does" edges toward "every case" logic. It isn't the umbrella phrase, so it doesn't clash in words, but it is an absolute. Tone check: calm, peer, no novices. Passes. "the most" should be "most", tighter.
- Verdict: ADVANCE with the turn. Strongest of the two. Never use the first sentence alone.

**Best 3 (marked *):**
- **"The one you reach for. Reach sooner."** Takes their own behaviour (reaching for it) and only moves it in time. No superlative, no instruction to change who they are. Peer tone. My pick.
- **"You already trust it. Every Tuesday too."** The Tuesday makes "ordinary day" concrete and gives the picture its brief. Slight risk of reading cute; keep the type calm.
- **"Every patient is the one that matters."** The emotional turn of Andres's line with the absolute moved onto the patient, not the device. Strongest heart, highest risk of drifting toward patient sentiment (the CD flagged that on C). Use as body or sign-off support, not as the headline for operators.

"Your first call, not your last" (13) is the cleanest literal answer to "Don't save the best for last" if Andres wants to keep the idiom's echo without the idiom.

## 3. Where it sits

Recommendation: **headline for territory C, "The grasp you already know", with that line as support.** Not its own territory.

Why:
- C is the CCI: what the team has proved becomes how it works. "Saved for the hard ones" is exactly what the team has proved. This insight is the reason C exists, not a new idea, so a separate territory would split one thought in two.
- A ("Two valves. One grasp.") is a device fact; B is the imager's view. Neither has a behaviour to change. Forcing a "reach sooner" line across all three would turn it into a campaign-wide instruction and flatten A and B.
- It can still appear across the campaign as a sales and body line ("Your first call, not your last"), just not as a shared headline.

**Recommended stack (corridor poster, C):**

> **The one you reach for. Reach sooner.**
>
> The grasp you already know.
>
> Your team chose PASCAL for the cases that asked the most of it. The same independent leaflet capture, the same control, the same hands. None of that changes on an ordinary list. What you've proved on the hard days is how you can work every day.
>
> Whichever valve. PASCAL.
>
> [TMTT umbrella lockup]

Alternate headline in the same stack, if CD wants Andres's line: **"Use it when it matters most. It always does."** with the same support and body. Then drop "every day" in the body to avoid stacking absolutes: "...is how you can work, on any list."

## 4. Two picture ideas for the corridor poster (the ordinary day, not the emergency)

1. **The board.** The cath lab whiteboard or a printed case list for a normal day: four or five routine names, times, nothing dramatic. Next to each, in the same marker, a small PASCAL tick or the device name written in by hand. Shot flat and close, cold morning light, a coffee ring on the corner. The headline sits above it. The team's own habit, written down, is the image. No heart, no anatomy, no drama.

2. **The hand on the handle, mid-list.** A tight crop of an operator's gloved hand resting on the PASCAL delivery system handle, relaxed, the grip of someone who has done this many times. Out of focus behind: the room clock at 9:40 a.m. and a lab tech mid-conversation, unhurried. It's the "honor the hands" reference from Getinge without the reverence: the point is how normal it looks. Calm palette, no red, no monitors in alarm.

Both keep the hard case off the page. Picture 1 fits "Reach sooner" best (it shows where they could reach). Picture 2 fits "The grasp you already know" best.
