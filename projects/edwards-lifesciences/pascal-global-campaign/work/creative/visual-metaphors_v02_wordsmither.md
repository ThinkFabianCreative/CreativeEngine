The test for every concept below: cover the headline. Does the image already make the point? Then uncover it. Does the line turn the point one notch further, or does it just say what we saw? Every concept is an episode of the CCI: *what a heart team has proved with PASCAL becomes how it works.*

## A · Precedent ("Your cases set the precedent.")

### 1. Verdict on v01
**Fails.** The long exposure of the room is atmosphere. With the headline covered it reads as "a cath lab, artfully shot". The meaning (habit lives in the room) sits entirely in the copy. The red trace is a decoration and risks reading as a path.

### 2. Concepts
The literal word is legal. Precedent = past rulings binding future ones. The image should *be* a piece of legal or record-keeping furniture whose contents are the team's PASCAL cases.

| # | Mechanism | Image | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Literal language | A law-report volume, "Reports of Cases", spine stamped with the hospital's program name and years; opened, the headnotes are PASCAL case summaries. | Floated as "law-report spines". Strong, but the spines alone read as a library. Needs the open page. |
| A2 | Literal language + context shift | A legal citation format used as the whole image: *St. Mary's Heart Team v. Mitral Regurgitation, 2024, PASCAL (14 cases)*, set like a case-law citation on a court record page. | Typographic, ownable, cheap to build per account. |
| A3 | Omission | Worn PASCAL drawer handle (floated). Wear tells the history. | Good, but wear is a prop; reads "used a lot", not "rules the next case". Judge: REWORK candidate, not top. |
| A4 | Omission | Foam tray impression in PASCAL's shape (floated). | Absence reads as "missing" or "out of stock". Ambiguous in the wrong direction. Drop. |
| A5 | Fusion | A judge's gavel whose head is the PASCAL implant. | Contrived. Fails exaggeration-vs-contrivance. Drop. |
| A6 | Literal language | Case reference card tucked into a brand-new procedure kit: "See: your case of March 4." The new case literally cites the old one. | Strong. The past case is inside the next one. |
| A7 | Reversal | The device IFU replaced by the team's own case log as the "instructions for use". | Risky: implies their log overrides labeling ⚑. Drop for compliance. |
| A8 | Context shift | Staff whiteboard heading "Standing rulings", listing ordinary scheduling items and one line: "Routine MR: see our PASCAL cases." | Overlaps Tuesday's board. Drop to keep territories distinct. |

### Pick 1: A6 · The cited case
- **Image:** A sealed, ordinary procedure tray on a steel table; clipped to the lid, a small index card in the team's own handwriting: "See: Case 0412, Mar 4." Nothing else.
- **Headline:** You've ruled on this before.
- **Why it adds:** The image shows a new case carrying a reference to an old one. The headline names the act: that reference is a ruling, and rulings bind. It turns a note into authority, which is the precedent idea and respects their standing.
- **Risk:** Case numbers and dates must be fictional or consented ⚑. The card must not suggest anatomy suitability is pre-decided; MLR may want "where appropriate" in support copy ⚑. Hard case is not shown; the cited case is unnamed, so it stays provenance.

### Pick 2: A2 · The citation
- **Image:** A clean page set exactly like a case-law report: citation line, court-style header, short headnote. The parties are the team and the disease; the holding is a one-line summary of their PASCAL history.
- **Headline:** Case law. Yours.
- **Why it adds:** Covered, the page already reads as "a legal precedent about a heart team". The headline adds ownership in three words; it does not explain the format.
- **Risk:** Legal parody can feel cold or litigious in a medical context. Keep it warm with real (consented) names and plain wording. "Yours" carries the whole idea; test with the core user.

**Slogan note:** "Your cases set the precedent." holds. The legal reading unlocks everything; don't change it.

## B · Tuesday ("Bring it to Tuesday.")

### 1. Verdict on v01
**Partly works.** The whiteboard with two PASCAL rows does land without the headline for someone who reads the board closely, but at a glance it is a schedule: a prop. "Nobody circled it" captions what we see. The metaphor is weak because Tuesday is only implied.

### 2. Concepts
Take "Tuesday" literally: the day of the week as an object, and "bring it" as physical carrying.

| # | Mechanism | Image | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | Literal language | A weekly pill-organizer style tray (S M T W T F S); in the T slot, a PASCAL device. | Clever but reads as medication. Drop. |
| B2 | Literal language | Tear-off desk calendar page: "TUESDAY", with nothing else on it but a small PASCAL shipping label stuck in the corner. | Clear and dry. The device "delivered to Tuesday". |
| B3 | Context shift | A PASCAL box with a FedEx-style delivery sticker: "Deliver: Tuesday. No signature required." | Strong wit. "No signature required" says ordinary, without saying easy. |
| B4 | Exaggeration | A scrub cap, lunch bag and coffee cup lined up on a locker bench; the PASCAL box among them, same level of importance. | Ordinariness by company. Good, but cluttered. |
| B5 | Reversal | Hard-case dramatic lighting (spotlight) shone on an empty table; the PASCAL box sits in the plain overhead light beside it. | Puts hard case in the frame as first beat. Breaks the rule. Drop. |
| B6 | Literal language | A week planner with "PASCAL" written in on Tuesday in the same pen as dentist and kid's game. | Weak; personal life confuses audience. Drop. |
| B7 | Omission | Hospital wall calendar where every day is ordinary; only Tuesday has a tiny red dot. Headline names it. | Too subtle covered. |

### Pick 1: B3 · The delivery label
- **Image:** Close-up of a PASCAL Precision shipping carton on a lab supply shelf; the label reads "DELIVER: TUESDAY" and "No special handling."
- **Headline:** No special handling.
  (or, if the label carries that text: *Your team already signed for it.*)
- **Why it adds:** Covered, the image says PASCAL is just another routine delivery. The headline "Your team already signed for it" adds the CCI: they accepted it long ago through their own cases; Tuesday is just where it goes now.
- **Risk:** "No special handling" could be misread as about device storage or care ⚑. Keep it the headline, not the label, if MLR objects. Must not read as "easy".

### Pick 2: B2 · The calendar page
- **Image:** A single tear-off calendar page, TUESDAY, blank, with a PASCAL lot sticker in the corner as if the team stuck it there.
- **Headline:** Nothing special. Exactly.
- **Why it adds:** The image gives the fact (PASCAL on an unmarked day); the headline flips "nothing special" from a dismissal into the goal.
- **Risk:** "Nothing special" can be pulled as a quote about the device. Pair with confident support copy. Light on proof; needs the support line to carry experience.

**Slogan note:** "Bring it to Tuesday." holds and is literal enough. Consider testing *"It's Tuesday's now."* as a variant: ownership verb, stronger with B3.

## C · Home Valve ("Both valves. Same team.")

### 1. Verdict on v01
**Works best of the three, but it's a format, not a metaphor.** Two identical lab photos labeled Mitral / Tricuspid read without the headline. "Spot the difference" is fun but captions the device of the layout. The image is still the team in a room; nothing is transformed.

### 2. Concepts
Take "home" literally: house, address, keys, family. Take "same team" literally: jerseys, rosters.

| # | Mechanism | Image | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Literal language | A house key ring with two keys labeled M and T, one keychain: the team's badge. | "Home" gets literal; both doors open with what you already carry. |
| C2 | Literal language | A team roster/jersey board: same names, same numbers, two fixtures listed (Mitral, Tricuspid). | Sport is common; feels generic. |
| C3 | Fusion | Heart anatomy drawn as a two-room floor plan; the same team badges hang on both doors. | Diagrammatic; risks implying same procedure ⚑. |
| C4 | Omission | Two lab ID badges, same photo and name; only the access line differs: "Mitral", "Tricuspid". | Simple, owned by the team, respects standing. |
| C5 | Context shift | A name-plate on the door of a second lab room, already engraved with the team's names. | Says "you belong here already". Strong. |
| C6 | Reversal | Welcome mat reading "Welcome back" outside a tricuspid room. | Charming; borders on cute. |

### Pick 1: C4 · The access badge
- **Image:** Two hospital ID badges lying side by side on a scrub top: same photo, same name, same title (Interventional Cardiologist). The only change: the access line, "Mitral TEER" on one, "Tricuspid TEER" on the other. ⚑
- **Headline:** Already cleared.
- **Why it adds:** Covered, it reads "same person, new access". The headline adds standing: they are not being granted a fresh start, they arrive cleared, which is the J2 belief. Respects credential barrier.
- **Risk:** "Cleared" could read as a regulatory or credentialing claim ⚑. Must be US-only with indication ⚑. Must not imply hospital credentialing is automatic.

### Pick 2: C5 · The name on the door
- **Image:** A frosted door to a cath lab with a fresh engraved plate: "Tricuspid", and below it the team's names, the same plate design as the mitral room next door.
- **Headline:** Your name's already on it.
- **Why it adds:** The image says belonging; the headline adds that the standing was earned before they got here: their PASCAL proof precedes them.
- **Risk:** Real names need consent ⚑. Room signage varies by hospital; may need localizing for EU.

**Slogan note:** "Both valves. Same team." is fine but doesn't feed an image; it describes. Test *"Same team. New door."* or *"Home on both valves."* to unlock C1/C5 literally.
