# PASCAL Global Campaign: visual metaphors v02 (DesignChamp)

CLIENT: Edwards Lifesciences · PROJECT: PASCAL Global Campaign · SOURCE SCOPE: PROJECT-LEVEL (brief v03, hero-ads v01, territories v01) · MODE: EXPLORE → DEVELOP · 2026-11-05

**Why v02.** Andres rejected the v01 key visuals as props and mood. He was right. Each one shows a place or an object and leaves the headline to do all the thinking. This round uses one test.

**THE TEST.** Cover the headline. Does the image on its own make an operator finish a thought within 3 seconds? If it doesn't, it fails. Methods: Pricken mechanisms (`pricken-idea-mechanisms.md`) and Barry's cold read, show don't tell, and SLIP IT (`barry-concept-art-direction.md` §14).

**Rules held throughout.** The hard case is never the subject or the first beat: where it shows up, it is provenance in the background. Every image is an episode of the CCI: *what a heart team has proved with PASCAL becomes how it works*. No next-gen device in action. No head-to-head. No arrows, roads, bridges or paths. No "every case". Edwards red, dark grey. No Capture Clarity here.

---

## A · Precedent: "Your cases set the precedent."

### 1. Current key visual, scored
v01 options: the program's case register open on routine cases with an earlier hard case cited in the margin, and the long-exposure lab.
- **Register: FAIL.** Cover the headline and you have a ledger. The idea is in the margin note, so you have to read it, and nobody reads it in 3 seconds. It also leans on the hard case to make sense.
- **Long exposure: FAIL.** It's a mood, "a team that's been here a long time". It says nothing about PASCAL, and nothing about something proved turning into routine.

### 2. Ideas
| # | Idea | Mechanism | Judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | *Floated:* in the TEER supply cabinet, the PASCAL drawer handle is rubbed bright among identical new ones | Omission (use is shown only by its mark) | **Strong.** Wear on a handle is a code anyone reads instantly: "this is the one they reach for." It shows the end state, not the hard case. Weakness: it shows habit more than precedent. |
| A2 | *Floated:* bound law-report spines that are the program's case logs | Analogy (case law) | **Weak.** Cover the headline and you get "archive". The legal link only exists because of the word "precedent", so the image follows the line. Cut. |
| A3 | *Floated:* foam tray holding a PASCAL-shaped impression just after a hand lifts it away | Omission | **Medium.** "Imply, don't reveal" works, but the image means "taken out" and doesn't say "routinely". Clinicians may also read packaging as a single use. Keep as a supporting frame. |
| A4 | The lab's own TEER protocol binder that falls open on its own at the PASCAL page, spine cracked there | Analogy (the book that opens where you use it most) | **Strong.** It's a universal code and needs no label beyond a tab. It also comes closer to precedent: the team's written practice has shaped itself around PASCAL. |
| A5 | A full stock shelf with one gap: the PASCAL slot, used down again | Omission | Clear in 3s, but "stock-out" reads as a supply problem. Risky for a manufacturer. Park. |
| A6 | The procedure-room clock and a stack of completed PASCAL case cards, ordinary times, nothing marked | Literal | A prop again. It fails the test. Cut. |

### 3. Best two
**A1 · The bright handle** (top pick)
- **Concept sentence:** The device a team trusts shows itself in the wear, not in a label.
- **Image:** Tight, raking-light still life of a real TEER supply cabinet in dark grey. Eight identical brushed-steel drawer pulls in a grid, all factory matte, except one rubbed to a bright, almost polished patch where thumbs land. The small printed drawer label under that one reads PASCAL. The other labels are unreadable at thumbnail size: neutral blanks or generic consumables, never competitor names. The only red is a hairline on the PASCAL label. No people, no hard case.
- **Why it works without words:** Wear marks are a visual language older than advertising, and they mean "used most". The operator finishes the thought on their own: *that's the one they go to*. The CCI is right there: proof turned into habit, shown as physical evidence.
- **Risk:** It shows habit and leaves out the cause. The headline and the chapter copy have to carry "your cases" (the provenance). Real cabinets may not hold devices like this, so check with Edwards or a lab before shooting, or present it openly as a constructed still life. The other drawers must never suggest a competitor device.

**A4 · The binder that falls open**
- **Concept sentence:** A team's practice wears itself into its own pages.
- **Image:** A worn lab binder lying flat on a dark-grey counter, fallen open by itself. The spine is cracked at one spot and the pages fan open to a section with a tab marked PASCAL in Edwards red. The facing page is dense with the team's own handwritten annotations, dated across months: routine entries, nothing circled. Other tabs (anatomy, imaging, closure) sit crisp and unthumbed.
- **Why it works without words:** "The book opens where you live" is a code everyone knows. Readers see that this team's practice now sits on PASCAL, and that the team wrote it themselves.
- **Risk:** It's close to the rejected register, because paper is paper. It survives only if the fall-open itself is the image, not anything written on the page. Treat the handwriting as texture: it must be illegible at ad size, with no case data.

## B · Tuesday: "Bring it to Tuesday."

### 1. Current key visual, scored
v01: the Tuesday whiteboard with "PASCAL" in red marker on routine rows. **FAIL.** Cover the headline and you have a schedule with a word on it. The thought rests on reading the board, so it's a document, not a metaphor.

### 2. Ideas
| # | Idea | Mechanism | Judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | A wall-mounted "In case of emergency" cabinet, door propped open with lab tape, a well-used handle, its PASCAL slot empty because it's out in use | Reversal (the emergency kit becomes the daily kit) | **Strong.** It reads in 1 second: "they stopped saving it." The emergency is provenance, and the image is about leaving it behind. |
| B2 | The good china, chipped from use, set for an ordinary weeknight dinner: paper napkins, takeout cartons | Context shift / analogy (the best things saved for occasions) | **Strong** cold read. Nobody in the world misses "don't save the best for special days." It's off category, so it needs the brand from the line. |
| B3 | A wall calendar where only one old date is circled red, and now every weekday has a small red tick | Literal | Reading it takes effort, and it's a diagram of the idea. Cut. |
| B4 | A Sunday-best suit on a hanger in the scrub locker, on a Tuesday | Context shift | Contrived. It points at clothes, not practice. Cut. |
| B5 | A "Reserved" table card folded flat and tucked under an everyday tray | Reversal | Subtle and dry, but too small at social sizes. Park. |
| B6 | A fire-extinguisher-style wall bracket labeled PASCAL, empty, its outline worn into the paint | Omission | It shares B1's idea with weaker clarity, and the fire code drags in the wrong drama. Fold into B1. |

### 3. Best two
**B1 · The emergency cabinet left open** (top pick)
- **Concept sentence:** What used to be kept for emergencies is now just out in use.
- **Image:** A flat, documentary shot of a real cath-lab corridor wall in dark grey. A familiar red-bordered "FOR EMERGENCY USE" wall cabinet. Its glass door stands open, wedged with a strip of white lab tape that has clearly been there for weeks. Inside, one empty slot with a small PASCAL label. The pull is shiny from use. In the soft background, ordinary daylight activity, nobody hurried. Edwards red only on the cabinet's border, never orange.
- **Why it works without words:** Everybody knows the emergency-cabinet code, and seeing it open as a matter of routine reverses that code at once: *this isn't kept for emergencies anymore*. The hard case appears only as the label the cabinet carries, never as an event. That keeps the image inside the provenance rule.
- **Risk:** MLR may read it as "PASCAL was an emergency device" or as unsafe storage. Also check that no real lab stores TEER systems this way. Fallback: present it as a stylized constructed set, not documentary. The empty slot must not read as "out of stock".

**B2 · The good china on a Tuesday**
- **Concept sentence:** The best thing in the house stops waiting for an occasion.
- **Image:** An overhead shot of a kitchen table on a weeknight, dark grey tabletop. Fine white china with a slim red rim, one plate with a small chip from years of use, holding ordinary food: noodles from a carton, a paper napkin, a phone face-down, a hospital badge on a lanyard dropped beside the plate. Nothing staged as celebration.
- **Why it works without words:** "We use the good plates on a Tuesday" completes itself for anyone. The hospital badge pulls it back to the operator's life.
- **Risk:** It's off category and close to a lifestyle ad. It can turn "PASCAL is the premium option" into a luxury signal. Andres has rejected mood before, so this one lives or dies on how hard the cold read lands. It works as a social or ABX opener, weaker as the lead key visual.

## C · Home Valve: "Both valves. Same team."

### 1. Current key visual, scored
v01: a mitral/tricuspid diptych, same lab and team, only the monitor changes. **FAIL.** Cover the headline and you get two near-identical photos, so it's a spot-the-difference that needs labels. "Same" is what the image is about, and sameness is invisible.

### 2. Ideas
| # | Idea | Mechanism | Judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | A heart cross-section in fine line, where the team's floor-tape marks (the X's where operator, imager and nurse plant) sit as the same small constellation at the mitral and at the tricuspid | Fusion (the lab floor + the anatomy) | **Strong.** Operators read anatomy in under a second, and a repeated pattern means "same" without a word. |
| C2 | The team's own name-stitched lead aprons on a hook rail by the second lab's door, hung in the same order as always | Literal / context shift | **Good.** Aprons are pure cath-lab code, and "our things are already here" means moved in, not visiting. Needs one cue for "the other valve". |
| C3 | Moving boxes labeled by role, already unpacked and flattened in a corner of a set-up room | Context shift (moved house) | It reads "new place, settled in" but leans on beginnings. Close to the "beginners" truth we want to deny. Park. |
| C4 | A house key that opens both doors | Analogy | Cliché, and a key-for-access trope. Cut. |
| C5 | The team's mugs, in their usual order, on the shelf of a second room | Context shift | Charming but small. A softer variant of C2 for social. |
| C6 | One shadow of the team cast across both sides of a heart model | Fusion | Contrived lighting, and it reads as sci-fi. Cut. |

### 3. Best two
**C1 · The same constellation, twice** (top pick)
- **Concept sentence:** The team brings its places with it to the other side of the heart.
- **Image:** A large, quiet anatomical line drawing of the heart in a four-chamber view, in pale grey on dark grey, in the style of an engraved medical plate. Over the mitral annulus, a small cluster of worn cath-lab floor-tape marks, the X's and L's a team puts down where each person stands, photographed real and comped in. Over the tricuspid, the identical cluster: same spacing, same wear, same scuffed corner on one strip. One mark in each cluster carries a thread of Edwards red (the operator's spot). No device.
- **Why it works without words:** An operator sees the two valves, then sees the same set-up repeated, and the thought lands before any copy: *we stand where we always stand, on both*. It also contradicts the "beginners again" fear without arguing with it. This is an episode of the CCI: what the team built on one valve becomes how it works on the second.
- **Risk:** Engraved anatomy plus collage can slide into a conference poster. It needs real tape shot at real wear, not graphics. "Both valves" is fine under the US tricuspid indication and the EU CE Mark. It must never pair with Capture Clarity, and US and EU legal versions stay separate.

**C2 · The aprons are already on the hooks**
- **Concept sentence:** You don't start over somewhere your things already hang.
- **Image:** A documentary close-up of a hook rail just inside a lab door. Five lead aprons, each with a stitched first name (fictional for the pitch), hung in an order a team knows by heart. On the door frame, a plain room sign the eye catches last, in the hospital's own type, e.g. "Structural · Tricuspid". The aprons are creased and lived-in. Dark grey wall, a red stitch on the operator's apron.
- **Why it works without words:** Hung-up personal gear is how anyone reads "we work here", and seeing it in the new room cancels "visiting" or "beginners".
- **Risk:** The idea needs the door sign, which is a word. It passes only if the sign is secondary and the aprons carry the image. In the cold read without the sign it reads "a team's lab", so this is the weaker pick. Names must be fictional or consented.
