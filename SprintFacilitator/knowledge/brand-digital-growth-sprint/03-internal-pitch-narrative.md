# Internal pitch narrative: the Growth Sprint as an entry point

For TRAFFIK leadership. Internal only. Client-agnostic; no client facts.

## The short version

We can open bigger relationships by selling a paid, fixed-fee sprint that answers one question honestly: does the client's brand and digital experience still reflect the business they've become? When the answer is "no, and here's where", the Blueprint names the work, and we are the team that already understands it. When the answer is "not really the website", we still get paid, we still earn trust, and we point them at the real priority.

The honesty is what makes the model work. It is not a disclaimer on top of it.

## How the work can unfold

```
Growth Sprint (paid, fixed)
   ↓  Blueprint + Digital Experience Roadmap
Decision gate 1: what does the roadmap say comes first?
   ↓
Brand Evolution          (if the brand lens found real gaps)
   refreshed expression, messaging, typography, photography / art direction,
   motion principles, digital design language. Evolution, not a rebrand,
   unless the evidence says otherwise.
   ↓
Decision gate 2
   ↓
Digital Experience       (if the experience lens found real gaps)
   IA, UX, content strategy, visual system, a Design Sprint on the riskiest
   page or flow, prototyping, design, build (native Instapage from an
   approved Figma spec, where that's the production route)
   ↓
Decision gate 3
   ↓
Market Reintroduction    (if there is something new worth telling people)
   launch campaign about what the business now does for customers.
   Closer to "we've changed, and our experience has caught up" than to
   "we redesigned our website".
   ↓
Ongoing marketing: content, media, iteration on live pages (Lean loop)
```

Each arrow is a decision the client makes with evidence in hand. The order can change: sometimes messaging comes before visuals, sometimes a small digital fix comes before any brand work. Some clients will take one workstream; some will take all of them. The account grows because each step earned the next.

## Why this works without direct-selling a redesign

- **It starts where the client's actual question is.** Most leadership teams don't wake up wanting a website; they sense the company has outgrown how it presents itself. The sprint names that feeling and tests it.
- **The client ranks the priorities.** When their own team places "customers can't find our newer products" in the top-right of the matrix, the case for the work comes from them and from their customers, not from our proposal.
- **We see the whole picture.** Strategy, brand, UX, content, build and media are usually bought separately and don't talk to each other. The Blueprint connects them, and the team that wrote it is the natural one to carry it.
- **It de-risks the big spend for them.** A fixed-fee sprint is an easy yes. A redesign scoped after the sprint is better scoped and less likely to overrun.

## The integrity line

The sprint has to be genuinely open to finding that the website isn't the priority. That is built into the method, not left to good intentions:

1. **The agenda never names a website.** It names a Digital Experience Roadmap whose columns include "Not now" and "Test first".
2. **The facilitator is neutral** and does not argue for a direction. TRAFFIK observers who want a particular outcome stay quiet in the room.
3. **Evidence and belief are marked separately** on every board. Priorities with no evidence become tests, not projects.
4. **Non-digital opportunities go on the same matrix** as digital ones: pricing clarity, sales enablement, product naming, onboarding, service.
5. **Customers are in the pre-work,** so the room isn't only the client's opinions plus ours.
6. **The sprint is priced to stand on its own.** Its value doesn't depend on selling what follows, so nobody on our side needs a particular answer.
7. **We don't script the "aha".** The strategy notes aimed for the room to conclude "our experience no longer reflects our business". We've removed that as a goal. If it's true, the room will get there; if we engineer it, the client will eventually feel led, and the roadmap loses its authority.

## If the website isn't the priority

It will happen. What we do:

- **Say so plainly in the Blueprint,** and recommend the real first move.
- **Offer what we genuinely do well** that fits the finding: messaging and positioning, sales and pitch materials, content, a light brand evolution, campaign and media, or a focused fix on one page or flow instead of a rebuild.
- **Refer out what we don't do** (for example pricing strategy, CRM or product changes), and say so.
- **Keep the relationship.** A client who hears "you don't need a new site yet" from an agency that could have sold one tends to come back to that agency when they do need one. That is a judgment from experience, not a statistic; we have no data on it yet, and we should start tracking it.

The commercial risk is real: some sprints won't lead to large projects. The trade is that the ones that do are better scoped, easier to approve and easier to defend, and the ones that don't still pay for themselves.

## What we should not do

- Don't forecast revenue per account or conversion lifts to the client. We have no evidence yet.
- Don't pre-scope the redesign before the sprint, or bring design comps to the session.
- Don't have the person who'd sell the redesign facilitate the sprint.
- Don't call the launch campaign a reintroduction unless the business has something new that customers would care about.

## What we need to decide internally

- Sprint fee and whether it is credited against follow-on work. A credit makes the yes easier but makes the sprint feel like a sales step; a standalone fee protects neutrality. Recommendation: standalone.
- Who facilitates (neutral, trained) and who plays evidence lead.
- Which client to pilot with, and how we'll measure whether the model works (sprints sold, follow-on rate, how often the answer was "not the website").
